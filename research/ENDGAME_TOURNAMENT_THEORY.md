## BOTTOM LINE UP FRONT

1. **Deadline-driven volume/volatility clustering in the final hours is close to a structural universal** across prediction markets and pari-mutuel/political betting markets — documented in the Iowa Electronic Markets (IEM), racetrack betting, and German political stock markets alike (Berg et al. 2008; Camerer 1998; Hansen et al. 2004). Expect wider spreads and noisier prints, especially in thin contracts, in the last 24–48 hours.
2. **With ~1,800 entrants, the textbook "desperate laggards gamble, calm leaders coast" story is the wrong mental model.** The most theoretically precise paper on exactly this question — a large-field, multi-period, top-position tournament model with an empirical test — finds that *in the final period, every manager maximizes risk except a leader with a sufficiently large lead, and that threshold lead-size shrinks as the number of competitors grows* (Chen, Hughson & Stoughton, 2025, *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis*). At N≈1,800, almost nobody is "safe." Expect broad-based, not just bottom-of-leaderboard, aggressive positioning into the close.
3. **Liquidity withdrawal by out-of-contention/risk-averse participants ahead of a scheduled uncertainty-resolution event is a robust, quantified pattern in adjacent markets** (pre-FOMC: Zhu 2023 BIS WP 1079; pre-earnings: Krinsky & Lee 1996; pre-bond-auction: Lou et al. 2024 BIS WP 1232). Expect fewer disciplined two-sided counterparties in SIG Cup's final days, concentrated in exactly the window where a shrinking set of still-live aggressive bettors is most active.
4. **Concentrated "whale" bets can and do distort prediction-market prices late in election cycles** (Intrade 2012 "Trader A," Polymarket's 2024 "French whale"), but the effect is conditional on market depth and arbitrage constraints: deep markets self-correct in minutes-to-hours (Rhode & Strumpf 2008; Tsang & Yang 2026), while **position-limited or thinly-arbitraged markets do not self-correct and distortions can worsen into the deadline rather than fade** (Zitzewitz 2026, NBER WP 35845 — verified directly by me).
5. **The US midterm vote-count calendar concentrates all of this into a narrow sliver of markets.** By the Nov. 4, 12pm ET lock, most races will already be called; genuine uncertainty (and therefore deadline-driven mispricing risk/opportunity) will be concentrated in slow-count states (historically California and similar mail-heavy states, which had 14+ uncalled House races a week after the 2022 midterms) and any runoff-eligible contests.
6. **Once mathematically out of contention for a discrete top-N prize, tournament theory's own logic says the marginal incentive to gamble collapses toward zero** (Moldovanu & Sela 2001; Becker & Huselid 1992 — "individuals who believe they may have no chance to finish in the upper echelons may simply give up"). This supports continuing to maximize EV rather than manufacturing variance — but Q4's prediction suggests a *complementary, still-EV-maximizing* opportunity: being one of the few remaining disciplined counterparties in the thin, still-live markets precisely because so many others will have disengaged.

## PART A — Q4: Aggregate Market Behavior in the Final Days (Primary Focus)

### A1. The mechanical deadline effect: volume and volatility cluster hard at the lock moment

This is the most robustly replicated pattern across every relevant market type I found, and it is largely *mechanical* (a function of order-book structure, not strategic intent):

- **Iowa Electronic Markets**: Berg, Forsythe, Nelson & Rietz (2008), *"Results from a Dozen Years of Election Futures Markets Research,"* Ch. 80 in *Handbook of Experimental Economics Results* (Elsevier) — directly fetched from the authors' University of Iowa page (`biz.uiowa.edu/faculty/trietz/papers/IEM Results.pdf`). IEM explicitly avoids using the raw election-eve closing price as its accuracy benchmark *because* "the thinning out of queues on the last day of trading" causes "substantial increases in the bid/ask spread and, in turn, the price volatility associated with a bid/ask bounce" (their footnote 8, on standing orders expiring at midnight). Quantified cost: across 15 elections, mean absolute forecast error was **1.49%** using a volume-weighted weekly average vs. **1.58%** using the raw last-price snapshot — the deadline-adjacent snapshot is measurably noisier. In 1996, they document a concrete case where "a large cash influx by new traders late in the campaign drove the price movement" away from the eventual true outcome in the final days.
- **Racetrack pari-mutuel betting**: Camerer (1998), *"Can Asset Markets Be Manipulated? A Field Experiment with Racetrack Betting,"* *Journal of Political Economy* 106(3): 457–482 (read in full, Caltech author repository). Baseline finding, independent of his manipulation experiment: **"about half the money is bet in the last 3 minutes before post time."**
- **Political stock markets**: Hansen, Schmidt & Strobel (2004), *Applied Economics Letters* 11(7): 459–463, on Berlin's 1999 election markets — **trading intensity in the hour before the publicly-reported 4pm price snapshot was roughly double** the intensity in neighboring hours.

**Implication for SIG Cup:** expect materially wider spreads and more erratic prints — especially on less-followed, lower-volume individual-race contracts — concentrated in the last trading hours before the Nov. 4 lock, essentially independent of whether anyone is behaving "irrationally."

### A2. Why "leaders coast, laggards gamble" breaks down at N≈1,800

This is the single most directly relevant theoretical result I found, and I personally downloaded and read the primary text.

**Chen, J., Hughson, E., & Stoughton, N., "Strategic Mutual Fund Tournaments,"** *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* 60(7), November 2025: 3344–3379, DOI 10.1017/S0022109024000590 (working paper version dated 2011, confirmed as the same paper via the published abstract). Verbatim from the abstract (I pulled the PDF and extracted text directly):

> "In the final period all managers maximize the amount of risk that they add to their portfolios with the exception of the leading fund. This fund locks in its advantage by reducing risk only if it has a sufficiently large lead. Empirically, we find that consistent with the theory, funds with larger leads decrease risk; **however trailing funds do not increase risks.**"

Critically, the model's comparative statics on what counts as "sufficiently large" (direct quote, p.2 of the WP, confirmed by my own extraction):

> "How large 'substantial' is depends on **the number of competitors**, how long opponents have to catch up and the maximum amount of risk they can take to do so. **The greater the number of competitors, [the] longer the time and the greater the risk, the less safe a lead is.** Finally, only for the special case of two competitors, is it always optimal for the leading fund to play safe, regardless of the size of her lead."

Two things to flag precisely: (a) their model is a *winner-take-all* tournament, not literally a top-3 structure — but a top-3 cutoff with ~1,800 entrants makes the "safe lead" problem *harder*, not easier, since there are effectively three moving targets to stay clear of, reinforcing rather than undermining the point; (b) their own empirical result is the mirror image of the classic Brown-Harlow-Starks "losers gamble" story — in their data, it's **leaders de-risking** (when safe enough), not laggards ramping up, that drives the pattern, because many trailing funds are already near-maximal risk throughout.

**Implication for SIG Cup:** with ~1,800 participants, very few people — including many who currently look like "leaders" relative to your bot's rank 145 — can be mathematically confident enough in their lead to rationally de-risk before lock. The theoretically-predicted aggregate picture is broad, sustained-to-intensifying risk-taking across most of the live-money portion of the field, not a clean bifurcation.

### A3. Concentrated/aggressive directional bets ("whales"): when they move markets and when they don't

- **Intrade 2012 (closest real precedent to "one trader distorting an election market late in the cycle")**: Rothschild, D. & Sethi, R., *"Trading Strategies and Market Microstructure: Evidence from a Prediction Market,"* *Journal of Prediction Markets* 10(1), 2016 (SSRN 2322420; studies the **2012**, not 2016, Obama-Romney Intrade market — corrected from my research brief). Per contemporaneous reporting on the paper (CNBC, Sept. 25 2013; Slate, Sept. 2013; BuzzFeed News), in the final **15 days** before the 2012 election, one trader ("Trader A") accounted for roughly **one-third of all money bet on Romney**, ~13,000 transactions, ~$4M in that window, trading exclusively pro-Romney even as results trended the other way, and **lost ~$4M over those two weeks (~$7M over the full cycle)**. His activity created a price "firewall" holding Intrade's Romney contract artificially elevated versus Betfair's equivalent — a firewall that **collapsed almost exactly at 9pm ET**, the moment Colorado (the last relevant swing state) closed its polls. The authors leaned toward a non-pecuniary manipulation motive (boosting campaign morale/media narrative) over genuine mispricing belief.
- **Polymarket 2024 ("French whale," Fredi9999/Theo4/PrincessCaro/Michie)**: well-documented by CNBC, Bloomberg, Reuters, Decrypt and CBS/60 Minutes (Oct–Nov 2024 reporting). A single French ex-bank trader deployed on the order of **$30–45M+** (on-chain-verified profit ~$47M across four accounts; higher ~$80M figures circulate but are not independently reconcilable on-chain), helping push Polymarket's implied Trump probability from a post-debate ~50/50 to **62–64%** by late October. France's gambling regulator (ANJ) opened a probe.
- **But market depth determines whether this matters**: Tsang, K.P. & Yang, Z., *"The Anatomy of a Blockchain Prediction Market: Polymarket in the 2024 U.S. Presidential Election,"* arXiv:2603.03136 (2026 preprint — title/existence confirmed directly by me via arXiv). Their estimated Kyle's λ (price-impact coefficient) **fell from a July 2024 spike of 0.53 to ~0.01 by October** — i.e., by October, a $1M net buy moved the implied probability only ~0.25 points, because the market had become too deep for concentrated flow to have outsized leverage. They also find pro-Trump/pro-Harris capital-inflow correlation jumped from 0.17 to 0.76 in early October (both sides got fresh capital simultaneously — more consistent with heterogeneous beliefs than pure one-sided manipulation).
- **The decisive variable is arbitrage/position constraints, not trader intent**: Zitzewitz, E., *"Limits to Arbitrage and Prediction Market Efficiency: the Case of PredictIt,"* NBER Working Paper 35845, published **October 5, 2026** (I fetched and read this directly — this is a genuinely contemporaneous paper, weeks old as of this report). Key finding, quoted directly from the text I extracted: on PredictIt (which historically capped positions at $850, raised to $3,500 after a 2025 relaunch), **"the longshot bias does not decline significantly as the election approaches"** — the opposite of Intrade, where Page & Clemen (2013) found it *does* decline as the election nears. Zitzewitz's explanation, quoted: *"arbitrageurs on Predictit face limits on downside risk that might become more binding over time, as arbitrageurs accumulate positions."* A concrete illustration from his Appendix B: during the contested Nov. 9–Dec. 10, 2020 period, PredictIt's eight different Trump-win-related contracts showed **average price dispersion of 5.6 percentage points** (vs. much less on Betfair), an arbitrage opportunity that several contracts' **5,000-trader position limits** prevented most participants from closing.
- **Cross-exchange evidence for 2024 generally**: Clinton, J.D. & Huang, T., *"Prediction Markets? The Accuracy and Efficiency of $2.4 Billion in the 2024 Presidential Election,"* SocArXiv preprint, 2025 (unreviewed — flagging this explicitly as not peer-reviewed). Across PredictIt/Kalshi/Polymarket/IEM in the final ~9 weeks: accuracy 93% (PredictIt), 78% (Kalshi), 67% (Polymarket); arbitrage opportunities existed on 62 of 65 days and — counter to naive efficient-market intuition — **"the opportunity for arbitrage actually grows in the last two weeks before converging to zero on Election Day."** They also report accuracy for the presidential race was *highest* more than a week out and *decreased* as Election Day approached.

**Implication for SIG Cup:** expect the headline, heavily-traded markets (e.g., national House/Senate control) to behave like deep-liquidity Polymarket — resilient to any single large bettor. Expect thin, individual-race, or otherwise under-followed contracts to behave more like PredictIt — capable of sustained, non-self-correcting mispricing if whoever is left trading them is capital- or attention-constrained.

### A4. Liquidity withdrawal ahead of scheduled uncertainty resolution

No paper studies this inside a prediction-market *contest* directly (flagged as a genuine gap by my research — see Part C), but the mechanism is one of the best-quantified patterns in market microstructure generally:

- **Krinsky, I. & Lee, J. (1996), "Earnings Announcements and the Components of the Bid-Ask Spread,"** *Journal of Finance* 51(4): 1523–1535. Around earnings announcements, the **adverse-selection share of the bid-ask spread rises from 46.6% (two weeks prior) → 59.5% (immediately pre-announcement) → 76.4% (event window)**, as order-processing and inventory-cost shares shrink correspondingly.
- **Zhu, X.S. (2023), BIS Working Paper 1079** (FOMC announcements): trading volume *falls* ahead of scheduled announcements as "discretionary liquidity traders" strategically withdraw to avoid trading against better-informed counterparties; this is estimated to explain **~⅓ of the pre-FOMC equity drift**.
- **Lou, D., Pinter, G., Üslü, S. & Walker, D. (2024), BIS Working Paper 1232** (UK gilts, 1997–2021): yields drift **0.3–0.5bp** (rising to 0.6–1.1bp with coincident bond supply) above normal in the pre-announcement window; mechanism is that **hedge funds (the active liquidity providers) reduce their share of liquidity provision**, replaced by more passive, less risk-sensitive capital.

**Implication for SIG Cup:** your own Q3 logic (confirmed by the literature in Part B3 below) implies that most of the ~1,800 entrants who are mathematically out of top-3 contention have textbook-predicted *zero* further incentive to actively, carefully quote two-sided prices in the contest's markets. If a meaningful fraction of the field disengages (or keeps only passive/stale positions) exactly as a shrinking set of still-live, aggressive bettors becomes most active in the final days, that combination is the textbook setup for exactly the kind of persistent, poorly-arbitraged dislocation Zitzewitz documents for PredictIt — not smooth, efficient, whole-market behavior.

### A5. Direct new evidence: a real top-3-bonus trading tournament (I verified this firsthand)

**Finet, A., Kristoforidis, K. & Laznicka, J. (2026), "Nonlinear Trading-Performance Patterns Among Novice Participants in an Incentivized Trading Simulation,"** *Econometrics* (MDPI) 14(2), Art. 30. This is about as close a structural analog to SIG Cup as exists in the literature: 134 students, **100,000 euros of virtual capital**, trading CAC40 securities, with **"bonuses for the three best portfolios"** creating "a tournament-style environment with continuous ranking feedback" (direct quote from the abstract, which I extracted from the PDF myself).

Their Table 7 ("Top-Performer Comparisons," read directly by me) gives exact figures:

| Variable | Top 10% (n=14) | Rest (n=120) | p-value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of transactions | 12.0 | 20.1 | 0.0003 |
| Average cash ratio | 44.1% | 61.7% | 0.0003 |
| Portfolio variability | 22,905.0 | 17,414.4 | 0.0057 |
| Average trade size (€) | 13,844.7 | 10,529.8 | 0.0639 |

Top performers traded **less often**, held **dramatically less cash** (i.e., were more fully invested/exposed), and had **~31.5% higher raw portfolio variability** than the rest — though in their full multivariate regression (controlling simultaneously for cash%, variability, and trade size), variability alone was *not* an independent significant predictor once those other factors were accounted for; the authors' own synthesis (verbatim): **"top-performing participants executed fewer transactions on average, held lower average cash, and displayed higher portfolio variability than the rest of the sample… This does not imply that top performers traded more on average."**

Most important for your question: the authors explicitly flag, as their own stated limitation, that they **"observe performance at the end… without modeling intra-session developments"** and list **"last-minute strategies"** as unstudied future work — i.e., even the newest, most structurally-matched paper available confirms that nobody has yet empirically clocked deadline-specific timing dynamics inside a real prize-for-top-3 trading contest.

**Implication for SIG Cup:** in the one real analog contest found, winning was about *conviction and exposure* (low cash, concentrated positions), not frantic trading activity — consistent with the idea that if you ever did want to add variance, the efficient way is through position concentration/sizing, not more transactions.

### A6. The SIG-specific wrinkle: the vote-count calendar concentrates the effect

This part is reasoned inference from public election-administration facts rather than an academic citation, but it's decision-relevant: by the Nov. 4, 2026, 12:00pm ET lock (the day after the Nov. 3 election), most races will already be called. Historically, close House/Senate races are *not* all resolved quickly — after the 2022 midterms, **14 House races were still uncalled roughly a week after Election Day**, concentrated heavily in California (whose mail-ballot counting rules routinely take 1–2+ weeks), and the Georgia Senate seat went to a December runoff, undetermined for over a month. If 2026 follows a similar pattern, genuine uncertainty — and therefore all the deadline-driven dynamics in A1–A4 — will be concentrated almost entirely in a relatively small number of slow-count or recount-eligible contracts, which are also likely to be the thinnest, least-covered markets in the whole contest. That combination (maximal remaining uncertainty + minimal remaining liquidity, right at the lock) is exactly the profile the literature above (Zitzewitz; Rothschild-Sethi; Krinsky-Lee/Zhu/Lou et al.) says is most prone to large, sustained, poorly-arbitraged mispricing.

### A7. Synthesis and actionable takeaway for Q4

Putting A1–A6 together, the best-supported expectation for SIG Cup's final ~9 days is **not** uniform market-wide chaos, but a *concentrated* pattern:
- Broad, sustained-or-rising risk-taking across most of the field (not just bottom laggards) through the lock, per large-field tournament theory (A2);
- A shrinking pool of disciplined, two-sided participants as out-of-contention entrants rationally disengage (A3/A4, consistent with Part B3 below);
- Mechanically noisier, wider-spread markets in the final hours (A1);
- All of this heavily concentrated in the handful of contracts tied to races genuinely still uncalled at lock time (A6) — which, by the Zitzewitz mechanism, may *not* self-correct the way deep markets like Polymarket's 2024 topline contract did.

**This suggests a concrete, still-EV-maximizing strategy shift distinct from "gambling to catch up": in your final days of trading, consider actively monitoring the thin, still-live, not-yet-called contracts specifically for the kind of stale/one-sided pricing this literature predicts, and being a disciplined counterparty there** — this does not require taking on the kind of tail risk that worsens your median outcome (per your own Q3 analysis), and is a direct market-structure implication of this research rather than a leaderboard-chasing gamble.

## PART B — Supporting Context (Q1–3, condensed)

### B1 (Q1): What tournament theory says about risk-taking, behind vs. leading

- **Lazear, E.P. & Rosen, S. (1981), "Rank-Order Tournaments as Optimum Labor Contracts,"** *Journal of Political Economy* 89(5): 841–864 (NBER WP 401). The foundational model treats effort, not variance, as the choice variable; its own footnote 1 notes that *if* risk-averse agents could choose variance, they'd lean toward **lower**, not higher, variance — the "gambling" literature is a later extension. Its Section IV on heterogeneous/mismatched contestants shows the incentive-sustaining prize spread required blows up toward infinity as the ability/standing gap widens, i.e., **effort incentives collapse for a badly mismatched contestant** absent handicapping.
- **Rosen, S. (1986), "Prizes and Incentives in Elimination Tournaments,"** *American Economic Review* 76(4): 701–715 (NBER WP 1668, directly read by my research agent). On weak contestants: **"there is a clear disadvantage to pessimism… An elimination design is a disadvantage to the timid because they are eliminated too quickly."** Optimism (even if inaccurate) has "positive survival value" for weak players.
- **Bronars, S. (1987), "Risk Taking in Tournaments,"** unpublished working paper, University of Texas (never found independently published — cited only via Hvide 2002 and Brown, Harlow & Starks 1996). The foundational "losers gamble" result: **"leaders in a sequential tournament have an incentive to 'lock in' their gains by playing safe, while followers choose a risky strategy."**
- **Hvide, H.K. (2002), "Tournament Rewards and Risk Taking,"** *Journal of Labor Economics* 20(4): 877–898, and **Hvide, H.K. & Kristiansen, E.G. (2003), "Risk Taking in Selection Contests,"** *Games and Economic Behavior* 42(1): 172–179. Critical nuance: Hvide's baseline symmetric model predicts both agents converge to *maximal* variance (not just the trailing one). Hvide & Kristiansen's asymmetric model shows **(safe,safe) can be the equilibrium** when the odds of a long-shot upset are small enough — i.e., **it is not always optimal for the weaker/trailing contestant to gamble**; sometimes playing safe dominates.
- **Chen, Hughson & Stoughton (2025, JFQA)** — see A2 above: in their multi-period, multi-competitor model, it's the **leader** who conditionally de-risks (only with a sufficiently large, field-size-dependent lead); trailing managers are typically already near-maximal risk, not newly "gambling."
- **Basak, S., Pavlova, A. & Shapiro, A. (2007), "Optimal Asset Allocation and Risk Shifting in Money Management,"** *Review of Financial Studies* 20(5): 1583–1621 (read directly from author's NYU Stern page). Critical finding: **"No risk shifting takes place when the manager is far behind or ahead of the benchmark"** — there is a *bounded* risk-shifting range; far enough behind, behavior reverts to normal (non-gambling) policy.
- **Basak, S. & Makarov, D. (2014), "Strategic Asset Allocation in Money Management,"** *Journal of Finance* 69(1): 179–217. Two-manager equilibrium: **"gambling behavior when… performances are close to the threshold"**, but **"chasing and contrarian behaviors when either manager substantially outperforms the opponent"** — a large gap changes the *qualitative* behavior, not just the magnitude.

### B2 (Q2): Empirical magnitude of deadline/year-end risk-shifting

- **Brown, Harlow & Starks (1996),** *Journal of Finance* 51(1): 85–110 (334 funds, 1976–91, read directly). Median risk-adjustment ratio: **1.20 (interim losers) vs. 1.12 (interim winners)**; logit-implied probability of increasing risk: **0.55 vs. 0.45**. Effect concentrated in the later/smaller-fund subsample, not stable across the full period.
- **Chevalier & Ellison (1997),** *Journal of Political Economy* 105(6): 1167–1200 (read directly, OCR'd from NBER WP 5234) — **the single most important nuance for your exact question**: the incentive to gamble is **non-monotonic**. For young funds, the payoff to increasing risk is **negative for funds far behind the market**, rises to a **local maximum about six points behind**, then falls to a minimum ~7 points ahead before rising sharply again far ahead. I.e., **the gambling incentive is strongest in the middle of the pack, not at the very bottom** — funds that are hopelessly far behind have a *risk-reducing*, not risk-increasing, incentive (for young funds specifically; older funds show a more uniformly positive incentive).
- **Busse (2001),** *JFQA* 36(1): 53–73: using **daily** instead of monthly return data, **"monthly results consistent with under-performers increasing their risk relative to better performing funds disappear"** — attributed to autocorrelation bias in the monthly measure.
- **Kempf & Ruenzi (2008),** *Review of Financial Studies* 21(2): 1013–1036 (read directly): in large fund families, **"the worst funds… increase risk by 1.83 percentage points more than the best funds"** (>10% of the sample's average risk of 17.7%); the direction **reverses in small families** (winners increase risk more there).
- **Kempf, Ruenzi & Thiele (2009),** *Journal of Financial Economics* 92(1): 92–108 (read directly): two **opposing** incentives — compensation incentive (behind ⇒ gamble) vs. **employment-risk incentive** (very poor performance ⇒ de-risk, to avoid a termination-triggering blowup). Which dominates flips with the **market regime** (bull vs. bear), not simply with how far behind a given fund is within a period.
- **Schwarz (2012),** *Review of Financial Studies* 25(3): 913–936: identifies and corrects a "sorting bias" that mechanically mimics tournament behavior; after correction, **reinstates** evidence that underperformers increase risk, and states this does **not** depend on market conditions (in tension with Kempf-Ruenzi-Thiele).
- **Genakos & Pagliero (2012),** *Journal of Political Economy* 120(4): 782–813 (41,550 observations, professional weightlifters, read directly) — the cleanest non-financial test, directly answering your question: risk-taking is **non-monotonic/inverted-U**, peaking at interim **rank 6**, then declining, and **flattening entirely below ~rank 17** ("statistically indistinguishable from rank-1 behavior"). Verbatim conclusion: **"professional athletes take greater risks when ranked close enough to the first athlete, but then revert to safer strategies when ranked lower."** Magnitude: rank-6 athletes announce **≥1.8kg more** than rank-1 (50% of the average stage-to-stage increase).
- **Becker & Huselid (1992),** *Administrative Science Quarterly* 37(2): 336–350 (NASCAR, read directly): prize-spread effects on finish position are large but show **"a remarkable erosion"** at low spreads; on safety specifically, crash/caution-flag effects only appear at **very high** spread levels (+10%+ caution laps per $1,000 of extra spread), while typical/low spreads show *more* careful driving with bigger spreads.
- **Carhart, Kaniel, Musto & Reed (2002),** *Journal of Finance* 57(2): 661–693 ("leaning for the tape," read directly): fund-index gains **+53.0bp on the last day of the year, loses −25.4bp the next day**; smallest-cap stocks surge **>100bp in the last half-hour** of Q4 trading; effect is strongest among the year's **top-10 performing funds** (+42bp/−29bp relative to others).

### B3 (Q3): Rational strategy once mathematically out of contention

- **Moldovanu & Sela (2001), "The Optimal Allocation of Prizes in Contests,"** *American Economic Review* 91(3): 542–558 (working paper read directly): footnote 16 — **"both the lowest and highest ability types have a zero probability of getting the second prize if there are at least three contestants."** Adding more paid ranks helps middle-ability contestants, not the very bottom.
- **Becker & Huselid (1992)** again, verbatim and directly on point: a steep "erosion of incentive effects" at low prize spread is explained because **"individuals who believe they may have no chance to finish in the upper echelons may simply give up."**
- **Brown, J. (2011), "Quitters Never Win: The (Adverse) Incentive Effects of Competing with Superstars,"** *Journal of Political Economy* 119(5): 982–1013 (WP read directly). When Tiger Woods entered a PGA event, higher-skill rivals' scores worsened ~0.8 strokes on average, but **"lower-skill players' scores appear unaffected"** — non-contenders' behavior is insensitive to further reductions in an already-negligible win probability, i.e., it reverts to/sits at baseline.
- **Metrick, A. (1996), "March Madness? Strategic Behavior in NCAA Basketball Tournament Betting Pools,"** *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 30(2): 159–172 (note: corrected venue from AER; read directly). Real money-motivated bettors systematically **under-exploit** the rising option value of differentiating from the crowd as pool size grows — they herd on "safe" favorites even when sub-optimal.
- **Poker ICM**: Gilbert, G.T. (2009), *"The Independent Chip Model and Risk Aversion,"* arXiv:0911.3100 (read directly) formally proves a **genuinely fair/neutral bet destroys tournament equity for every stack size** — the common "short stacks should embrace variance" heuristic is really about fold-equity/positive-chip-EV, not variance-seeking for its own sake.
- **Hunter, Vielma & Zaman (2016), "Picking Winners Using Integer Programming,"** arXiv:1604.01455 (DFS large-field tournaments, read directly): the rigorous framework is to **"maximize mean subject to a variance floor, never variance alone."**
- **Holmström, B. (1999), "Managerial Incentive Problems: A Dynamic Perspective,"** *Review of Economic Studies* 66(1): 169–182 (NBER WP 6875, read directly): baseline career-concerns logic implies a risk-averse agent facing a *symmetric/uninformative* lottery about fixed ability should **avoid**, not seek, extra variance — pure "reputation" is not automatically a reason to gamble.
- **BUT Zwiebel, J. (1995), "Corporate Conservatism and Relative Compensation,"** *Journal of Political Economy* 103(1): 1–25 (abstract read directly): **"very high and very low ability managers are more likely to undertake superior innovations than those of average ability"** — because conforming to the pack guarantees a perceived-bad performer is correctly identified as bad, while deviating creates a chance of being mistaken for a lucky top performer. This is the most literature-grounded case for *some* rational variance-seeking even absent a cash prize, if you place residual value on relative leaderboard position.

**Synthesis for Q3**: the weight of the literature (Moldovanu-Sela's zero-probability footnote, Becker-Huselid's "give up," Genakos-Pagliero's flattening at the bottom, Basak-Pavlova-Shapiro's "no risk shifting far behind," Holmström's baseline) supports reverting to ordinary EV-maximization once a top-N prize is realistically unreachable. A defensible exception (Zwiebel; Hunter-Vielma-Zaman's "variance floor, not variance alone") exists only if (a) you have an actual edge to pair with the variance, not a pure coin-flip, and (b) you place genuine independent value on relative leaderboard rank itself (e.g., visible/reputational value, per Zwiebel) — in which case a *bounded* tilt, not an all-or-nothing longshot push, is what the theory supports.

## PART C — Gaps, Caveats, and Honest Uncertainty

- **No study directly examines deadline-specific timing dynamics inside a real, prize-for-top-N trading contest.** Even the closest analog (Finet, Kristoforidis & Laznicka 2026) explicitly flags "last-minute strategies" as unstudied. Everything in Part A is a reasoned synthesis across adjacent literatures (fund tournaments, prediction-market microstructure, scheduled-event liquidity), not a single direct empirical test of "contests like SIG Cup in their final days."
- **Taylor (2003)** and **Goriaev, Nijman & Werker (2005)** (both cited in B2) could only be corroborated via converging secondary summaries, not independently fetched primary text (paywalled/blocked on every attempt) — treat with moderate rather than high confidence.
- **Bull, Schotter & Weigelt (1987)** experimental findings are similarly secondary-sourced (JSTOR-blocked); widely cited and probably reliable, but not independently verified by primary text.
- **Rothschild & Sethi's own paper** was characterized via high-quality contemporaneous journalism (CNBC, Slate, BuzzFeed News) rather than a direct fetch of the SSRN/journal text itself.
- **Clinton & Huang (2025)** is an unreviewed SocArXiv preprint, not peer-reviewed; treat its specific accuracy/arbitrage percentages as credible but provisional.
- **No Betfair in-running/in-play liquidity paper by the specific author combination (Smith/Paton/Vaughan Williams) could be located** — this may not exist under that exact author trio; a genuine search gap, not a confirmed absence of such research generally (e.g., Schnytzer & Weinberg have related in-play work not covered here).
- I did **not** verify SIG Predictions Cup's own rules beyond a confirmatory web search (confirmed: 100,000 "SUSQies" starting capital, Oct 1–Nov 4 2026 window, $30k/$5k/$2.5k prizes, ties split pooled prize money, bot trading explicitly permitted) — this is background grounding, not the academic focus of the report.
- The "vote-count calendar" argument in A6 is my own reasoned inference from public election-administration reporting (PolitiFact, NBC News on the 2022 midterms), not a peer-reviewed citation — flagged as such.
