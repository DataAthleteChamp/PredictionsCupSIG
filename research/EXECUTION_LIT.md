## 1. Queue position and order splitting

**The literature's actual result (Cont & Kukanov 2017, *Quantitative Finance* 17(1):21-39, arXiv:1210.1625)** **[verified primary text]**: the paper formulates expected execution at a price level as
```
A_k(L_k) = (ξ_k − Q_k)⁺ − (ξ_k − Q_k − L_k)⁺
```
where `Q_k` = volume already queued ahead of you, `ξ_k` = total marketable volume that will arrive at that level before your horizon, and `L_k` = your posted size. In the proof of their Proposition 2 they show explicitly that this function is **concave in `L_k`** — i.e., diminishing returns to posting more size, exactly the saturating curve you measured (100% → 77% → 32% → 18%). Their explicit single-exchange solution and multi-exchange KKT conditions (eq. 21-24 in the appendix) are stated entirely in terms of `Q_k` and the distribution of `ξ_k` — **the model requires observing queue depth ahead of you at every candidate price**. You don't have that, so the closed-form solution is not directly computable. Their own fallback for this exact situation is a **Robbins-Monro-type stochastic-approximation algorithm** that learns the optimal static split purely from realized fill outcomes, without ever observing the book — structurally close to what you're already doing with your 2,300-fill dataset, except it typically needs far more iterations to converge than you have (arXiv:1210.1625, §3.2; summarized in Cont & Kukanov 2017, ideas.repec.org/a/taf/quantf/v17y2017i1p21-39.html).

**Does splitting one order into several at the same price help under FIFO?** The honest answer, derivable directly from the formula above: **no**, and this is not a minor caveat — it's structural. `A_k(L_k)` depends only on the *total* size `L_k` posted at that queue position, not on how it is partitioned into child orders submitted at the same instant. If you split a 320-share order into 4×80 at the same price and time, the aggregate expected fill across all four children is mathematically identical to the expected fill of one 320-share order — your combined block still has the same `Q_k` ahead of it and the same `ξ_k` hits it from the front. What splitting *does* change is pure overhead: more messages (consumes your rate-limit/order-count budget for zero expected-fill benefit), more cancel/replace events, and (if your venue has per-account order caps, as most do) fewer total rungs or markets you can quote with the same budget. This differs from **pro-rata** matching venues, where splitting into many small clips genuinely does help because many venues guarantee each order a minimum lot — but you explicitly said FIFO, so that mechanism doesn't apply.

**What your fill-ratio-by-size data is actually telling you:** your own description — "Spearman(order size, fill size) only +0.305, so the market is flow-capped" — is the right diagnosis, and it is the signature the Cont-Kukanov concavity predicts: there is a roughly fixed amount of marketable flow available at each rung regardless of how much you post, so posting >160-320 shares buys you almost nothing incremental. **The actionable conclusion is not "split," it's "shrink."** Cap size per rung near your empirically-observed saturation point (~80-160 shares) and redeploy the freed order-count/margin budget into *more rungs or more markets*, where the marginal fill probability is still close to 1.0, rather than into bigger clips at rungs that are already flow-saturated. Splitting and shrinking are different operations and only one of them is supported by the math.

One secondary, non-fill-rate argument for smaller clips does survive: **variance reduction** — a single 320-share fill landing in an adverse move is lumpier risk than four 80-share fills spread over time, purely as a P&L-smoothing consideration, not an expected-value one. Worth noting but secondary.

## 2. Repricing frequency — your plausible biggest lever

**What the classical market-making control literature says:** Avellaneda & Stoikov (2008, *Quantitative Finance* 8(3):217-224), Guéant, Lehalle & Fernandez-Tapia (2013, *Mathematics and Financial Economics* 7:477-507, arXiv:1105.3115), Fodra & Labadie (2012, arXiv:1206.4810), and Guilbaud & Pham (2013, *Quantitative Finance* 13(1):79-94) all solve for the optimal quote as a function of inventory, volatility, and order-arrival intensity — **but every one of these models assumes quotes can be updated continuously and costlessly**. None of them has a term for "cost of losing queue priority on cancel/replace" or "message-rate budget." This means the canonical HFT market-making literature is **silent on your actual question** — it's answering a different problem (how aggressive should my quote be) not yours (how often should I refresh it). This is an important negative result in itself: there is no off-the-shelf closed-form "optimal reprice interval T\*" in this literature, contrary to what you might expect **[verified via search synthesis of GLFT/Fodra-Labadie/Guilbaud-Pham structure; I did not find a counter-example in several searches]**.

**The literature that actually addresses your trade-off is the queue-position-valuation line of work:**
- **Moallemi & Yuan (2016/2017), "A Model for Queue Position Valuation in a Limit Order Book,"** Columbia Business School Research Paper No. 17-70, ssrn.com/abstract=2996221 **[secondary source — SSRN blocked fetch, relying on abstract summary]**: decomposes queue-position value into a static component (spread capture minus adverse selection, which *worsens* deeper in the queue) and a dynamic optionality component, and states that for large-tick instruments the value of queue position "can be as significant as the bid-ask spread itself." Since your tick (0.005 on a 0.005–0.995 range) is a large fraction of price especially away from 0.50, this is directly applicable — queue position is not a rounding error for you, it's comparable in size to your whole edge.
- **Donnelly & Gan (2018), "Optimal Decisions in a Time Priority Queue,"** *Applied Mathematical Finance* 25(2):107-147 **[secondary source — not independently fetched]**: builds an explicit cancel-vs-hold model calibrated to Nasdaq data and reports that a state-dependent cancellation policy (conditioning on current queue rank and market state) beats a naive/fixed policy by **~2.5% higher return at equal risk, or ~8.8% lower risk at equal return**. This is a real, but modest (not 2x), quantified number — treat it as a lower bound on what a *smarter trigger* buys you over a *blind timer*, not as your expected total improvement.
- **Ayyar (2026), "Queue Priority as Adverse-Selection Exposure,"** aryanayyar.com/queue-priority-adverse-selection-exposure.pdf **[verified primary text — but note: single-author, posted June 2026 on a personal site, not yet peer-reviewed or indexed on SSRN/arXiv as far as I could find; treat as a plausible, rigorous-looking framework rather than an established result]**. This paper formalizes exactly your cancel-and-replace situation: "the only same-price repositioning available to an occupied rank is to cancel and re-enter **at the back of the displayed queue**" (§6.4), and proves a **repost-stability condition**: reposting only pays off if your reposting cost is below a "surrendered block premium" `C*_k`. Its worked numerical example (Table 1) found the *front rank* can have a **negative** priority premium (Δ₁ = −0.099) while an interior rank (rank 3 of 5) is the value-maximizing position — i.e., being at the very front of a queue is not always best because the front captures the earliest (and most toxic) fills, while deeper ranks let other resting orders "absorb" the first, most-informed hits. **This is precisely the pattern in your own rung data**: your 3-tick rung (closest/most "front-like") takes 93.2% of fills but the lowest edge (+0.00405), while your 6-tick rung takes only 6.8% but the highest edge (+0.01883) — a **4.6x edge difference by rung depth**, which is a far bigger and more statistically solid effect than anything else in your dataset, and it's exactly what this framework predicts.

**Does cancel-and-replace quantifiably lose priority?** Yes, unambiguously and by construction: under FIFO, cancelling and resubmitting always sends you to the back of the queue at whatever price you repost at (Ayyar 2026 §6.4; this is also just the definition of FIFO, not a modelling assumption). The open question the literature actually debates is not *whether* this happens but *whether it's worth it anyway* given adverse selection — and the answer from Donnelly & Gan and Ayyar is "sometimes, conditionally," never "always on a fixed clock."

**What I'd actually do, given no closed form exists:** replace the blind 300-second timer with an **event-triggered reprice**: (a) reprice immediately if the mid has moved ≥1 tick from the price your rung was anchored to (computable from top-of-book alone, zero new data needed), with (b) a shorter backstop max-dwell timer (try 60-90s) as a ceiling. This is directly testable with your existing fill-logging pipeline — change the trigger condition, not the architecture, and re-measure the same edge-by-dwell-time curve you already compute.

**Why I rank this as your biggest lever:** your own numbers put the "5 minute" mark on the edge-decay curve (+0.00217) almost exactly at your current reprice interval (300s = 5 min) — meaning *by the time you'd reprice anyway*, you've already given up the majority of the edge you'd have captured with a fresher quote, and you're accumulating exposure into the region where edge is heading negative. Shortening the loop should mechanically shift your fill-weighted-average edge back toward the fresher, higher part of the curve. I want to be precise about confidence here: **no paper gives me a "2x" number for your specific setup** — that estimate is my own inference from combining your decay curve with the queue-priority literature's qualitative direction, not a transplanted result. It is however the only candidate lever backed by *both* an independent theoretical literature *and* a strong pattern already visible in your own data, which is why it ranks above signal-based ideas below.

## 3. Adverse selection and quote skewing

**Order-flow imbalance (Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov 2014, "The Price Impact of Order Book Events," *J. Financial Econometrics* 12(1):47-88, arXiv:1011.6402)** **[verified primary text + methodology confirmed via Xu, Gould & Howison 2018 restatement, arXiv:1907.06230]**: OFI is defined purely from **best bid/ask price and size changes between consecutive book events** — it does *not* require trade-side/aggressor classification, hidden depth, or anything beyond Level-1 data. That is good news: it is computable from exactly the top-of-book feed you already have. Their empirical result, from NYSE TAQ data on 50 random S&P 500 stocks (April 2010): regressing 10-second mid-price changes on OFI gives **R² ≈ 65%**, versus **R² ≈ 32%** for simple trade imbalance, with the OFI slope coefficient significant in 98% of stock/period regressions and the intercept significant in only 10% (i.e., a no-intercept linear model `ΔP ≈ β·OFI` fits well). The paper explicitly states the relationship is "stable across time scales," but the headline 65% figure is measured at a **10-second** horizon — thirty times shorter than your 300-second loop. The qualitative finding most relevant to you: **the price-impact slope is inversely proportional to market depth** (arXiv:1011.6402 abstract), meaning in a thin book like yours, the same OFI reading should imply a *larger* price move than in a liquid S&P 500 name — the signal-to-noise ratio for OFI could plausibly be *better* for you precisely because you're thin, but I found no paper testing this on anything resembling your contract type, so this is informed extrapolation, not a verified transplant.

**Multi-level OFI**: Xu, Gould & Howison (2018/2019, *Market Microstructure and Liquidity*, arXiv:1907.06230) **[verified primary text for methodology]** show that extending OFI to deeper book levels improves predictive power further; a follow-up line of work (cited as Kolm & Westray, SSRN 4568641) reportedly pushes R² from ~55% (best-quote-only) to ~80% (10 levels deep) **[secondary source only — Springer gave me a 406 error, could not verify directly, treat this number with real caution]**. The directionally-solid takeaway, which I can state with much more confidence: **the incremental signal from levels 2+ is exactly the part of OFI you cannot compute without a depth feed** — top-of-book OFI alone captures the majority but not all of the available signal.

**Microprice (Stoikov 2018, "The Micro-Price: A High Frequency Estimator of Future Prices," *Quantitative Finance* 18(12):1959-1966)** **[verified primary text, full paper extracted and read]**: this is your best-supported idea for a better fair-value anchor, for one specific reason — the paper states explicitly **"this model can be fitted to Level-I data"** (i.e., bid/ask price and size history only — no depth required). Concretely it's the limit of a sequence of expected-mid-price adjustments conditioned on (imbalance, spread) via a fitted Markov chain, and in the paper's own empirical tests the micro-price adjustment is **bounded at less than half the quoted spread**. Two important caveats direct from the paper, both cutting against you: (1) "as the spread widens, the slope of the micro-price as a function of imbalance lessens... when the spread is 4 ticks, the micro-price is indistinguishable from the mid-price and the imbalance seems to have no additional information" — you quote 3, 4 and 6 ticks *back from mid*, implying your effective spreads are probably wide by this paper's standard, which directly predicts a muted signal for you; (2) the right prediction horizon is asset-specific and scales with tick-size-relative-to-price — the paper found a 3-minute horizon was needed for a "large tick" stock (BAC) versus 10 seconds for a "small tick" stock (CVX), and your 0.005 tick on a 0.005–0.995 range is arguably a more extreme large-tick case than their BAC example. This is consistent with your own observation that edge is still positive at 5 minutes — it hints your natural "price discovery horizon" probably is on the order of single-digit minutes, roughly where your current loop already sits, for this particular reason (not necessarily for the reprice-priority reasons in §2).

**Supporting context specific to your asset class:** a 2026 Federal Reserve working paper on Kalshi (Diercks, Katz & Wright, "Kalshi and the Rise of Macro Markets," FEDS 2026-010) **[verified primary text extracted locally]** notes "midpoints of bid-ask spreads seem to introduce additional issues due to occasionally large spreads on tail outcomes" — i.e., wide spreads near 0/1 are a known feature of this exact contract type, reinforcing the Stoikov caveat above. A very recent large-scale Polymarket microstructure study ("The Anatomy of a Decentralized Prediction Market," arXiv:2604.24366) **[verified primary text]** independently finds the same "longshot premium" in quoted half-spread, and separately finds that trade-direction inferred purely from public order-book feed changes agrees with true (on-chain) aggressor side only ~59% of the time, barely above a coin flip — a caution specifically about **trade-classification-based** signals (VPIN-style, Lee-Ready-style) on this class of venue, though it does *not* undermine OFI itself, since OFI never needs an aggressor label, only quote deltas.

**Inventory skewing:** Guéant, Lehalle & Fernandez-Tapia (2013) give the standard closed-form result that optimal bid/ask should be centered on a reservation price shifted away from mid in proportion to `risk_aversion × volatility² × inventory × time-to-horizon`, not symmetric around mid **[formula structure confirmed via secondary search synthesis; I was not able to independently re-derive the exact algebraic coefficients this session, so treat the precise constants with some caution even though the qualitative structure is textbook-standard]**. A 2025 paper, Barzykin, Bergault, Guéant & Lemmel, "Optimal Quoting under Adverse Selection and Price Reading" (arXiv:2508.20225) **[verified primary text]**, adds a real caution: mechanical inventory skewing can be detected by "skew sniffers" who infer your inventory direction from your quote asymmetry and trade against you, so skewing has a hidden cost at scale. That paper's context is dealer/RFQ markets responding to direct requests, not a resting-CLOB maker like you, so the mechanism may not transfer directly — but for a participant your size, the more basic point stands: skew-sniffing risk scales with how much flow is specifically watching *you*, which is plausibly low for a small participant across 80 simultaneously-quoted niche markets. I'd rate inventory skewing as a plausible but **not evidence-backed-for-your-situation** idea — it solves an inventory-risk problem that your setup (many small, presumably short-dated, resolving contracts) may not even have much of, compared to a single-instrument continuous market maker.

## 4. When not to quote

**Toxicity-based stand-aside**: Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara (2012), "Flow Toxicity and Liquidity in a High-Frequency World," *Review of Financial Studies* 25(5):1457-1493 **[secondary source — not independently fetched this session, but this is a canonical, extremely well-known paper]** introduce VPIN (Volume-Synchronized Probability of Informed Trading), built from volume-bucketed order imbalance rather than clock time, and show it spiked before the May 2010 Flash Crash as market makers who were being adversely selected progressively withdrew. This is the standard published "stand aside" filter in the literature, but building it requires a volume-clock and trade-side classification pipeline you don't currently have — and per the Polymarket finding above, feed-based aggressor-side inference may be unreliable on a CLOB-style prediction-market feed specifically, so this would need validation against whatever ground truth your venue actually exposes before trusting it.

**Scheduled-news stand-aside**: there's a deep, consistent empirical literature (e.g., work on liquidity withdrawal before scheduled U.S. macro announcements, and Treasury-market order-flow amplification studies such as a 2025 Federal Reserve FEDS note, federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/order-flow-imbalances-and-amplification-of-price-movements-evidence-from-u-s-treasury-markets-20251103.html) **[secondary source, general literature consensus, not individually verified this session]** showing spreads widen and displayed depth falls mechanically ahead of scheduled releases as market makers defensively pull size. Given your contracts are explicitly event/outcome contracts, this is very likely to transfer directly — standing aside (or at minimum widening/thinning) around your markets' known resolution-relevant news windows is a well-supported, cheap-to-implement rule, contingent only on you having a calendar of relevant events per market.

**Your AT/INSIDE-mid finding is the textbook "picked-off" signature, and I think it's actually your cheapest, highest-confidence fix.** The classic theoretical grounding is old and extremely well established: Copeland & Galai (1983, "Information Effects on the Bid-Ask Spread," *Journal of Finance* 38(5):1457-1469) and Foucault (1999, "Order Choice and the Cost of Free Option to Trade," *Journal of Financial Markets*) both formalize a resting limit order as a **free option** that informed traders exploit exactly when the quote is stale relative to true value — which is structurally identical to what "quoting at/inside mid" means for you. Given your described setup (rungs always placed *below* mid by construction), a fill classified as "at or inside the mid" almost certainly means **the live mid moved down and through your resting price sometime during your 300-second dwell window, and you got hit right as — or after — that happened**, rather than you deliberately choosing to quote there. That makes this finding not a separate "fourth quoting mode" to avoid, but the **extreme tail of the exact same staleness problem as §2**, caught in the act: P(>0)=0.000 across your whole sample is about as unambiguous a signal as 2,300 fills can produce, regardless of your stated power limits for 20% effects — a result where literally none of the observations have positive sign is not a marginal-power problem. **Concrete fix, implementable today with data you already have:** add an immediate-cancel trigger that fires whenever the live mid reaches or crosses a resting rung's price, rather than waiting for the scheduled 300-second refresh. This requires no new data feed (you already must be sampling mid to reprice at all) and directly operationalizes a finding you've already proven to yourself.
