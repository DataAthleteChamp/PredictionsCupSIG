# Research Report: Academic Literature on Forecasting US Midterm Elections — Models vs. Markets

## Summary

Across ~45 sources (peer-reviewed papers, primary methodology documents, and verified journalistic accounts), the evidence does **not** support a clean "markets usually win" rule. The historical scorecard across 2016/2020/2022/2024 is genuinely mixed — markets clearly lost to models in 2022, clearly beat the leading model-based forecasters' point estimates in 2024, and produced a split decision in 2016/2020. The current ~10-point Senate gap is mechanically explainable: Decision Desk HQ's own published methodology shows it explicitly *down-weights* market prices (a capped, volume-scaled blend, not a 1:1 read), while fundamentals (Trump net approval −5 to −8, a 3.9-point Democratic generic-ballot lead) point to a real but moderate Democratic edge, not a 65%+ one. On methods: Bayesian hierarchical/MRP and correlated Monte Carlo are not window dressing — they solve a real, well-documented failure mode (underestimating cross-state correlation), but the correlation parameter itself is usually a hand-tuned scalar, and getting it wrong has sunk serious models twice in the last three cycles. Polling error is large, fat-tailed-ish, and correlated across states — roughly double the naive margin-of-error. Building a better-than-Polymarket model in two weeks is very unlikely; building one that flags *when* the market is probably wrong is plausible and is the honest, defensible target.

## 1. Models versus markets — the decisive question

### 1a. The classic debate (established, with a live, unresolved dispute at its center)

- **Berg, J.E., Nelson, F.D., & Rietz, T.A. (2008). "Prediction market accuracy in the long run." *International Journal of Forecasting*, 24(2), 285–300.** https://pdf.stafforini.com/berg-2008-prediction-market-accuracy.pdf (PDF verified directly). Comparing IEM prices to 964 polls over five presidential cycles (1988–2004), they report the market closer to the eventual result 74% of the time, with the market's advantage growing the further out from election day (final-week market error ~1.3 points vs. ~1.6 for polls, gap widening at longer horizons). **This is the canonical pro-market citation.**

- **Erikson, R.S., & Wlezien, C. (2008). "Are Political Markets Really Superior to Polls as Election Predictors?" *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 72(2), 190–215.** https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfn010 (abstract/summary verified via academic.oup.com and JSTOR listings). Their central argument, **directly contesting Berg/Nelson/Rietz**: raw market prices are being compared unfairly against same-day trial-heat polls; once polls are "properly discounted" using known regularities (e.g., winner-take-all market prices overstate uncertainty relative to a vote-share-based poll projection), **poll-based forecasts beat market prices**, especially for winner-take-all contract pricing. They argue markets anticipate more campaign-driven volatility than actually materializes.

- **Status: unresolved, not merely "contested" — I could not find a direct, peer-reviewed rebuttal to Erikson & Wlezien (2008) specifically.** I attempted to find one explicitly; a search tool returned what it labeled a "sample rebuttal... in the spirit of Berg and Rietz" — **I am flagging this explicitly as fabricated/hypothetical, not a real citation**, and have excluded it. The closest things to a real-world "answer" to Erikson & Wlezien are (a) Berg/Nelson/Rietz's own prior empirical case (above), which predates the critique and doesn't address the debiasing argument, and (b) Arrow, K. et al. (2008), "The Promise of Prediction Markets," *Science*, 320(5878), 877–878, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1157679 — a 21-author position piece (Arrow, Hahn, Hanson, Ledyard, Milgrom, Shiller, Snowberg, P. Tetlock, Varian, Wolfers, Zitzewitz, et al.) broadly defending prediction markets' value and arguing for *reduced* regulatory barriers, published the same year, but it is a general advocacy piece, not a methodological rebuttal of the debiasing argument. **Flag this as a genuine gap**: the debiasing critique has, as far as I could verify, not been directly and rigorously answered in print.

- **Rothschild, D. (2009). "Forecasting Elections: Comparing Prediction Markets, Polls, and Their Biases." *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 73(5), 895–916.** https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/73/5/895/1868587; preprint at https://stafforini.com/works/rothschild-2009-forecasting-elections-comparing/ (abstract fetched directly). Using 2008 presidential and Senate races, Rothschild **debiases both sides** (not just markets) for their respective known biases (favorite-longshot bias in markets; discounting/house effects in polls) before comparing. Finding: *"early in the cycle and in not-certain races, debiased prediction market-based forecasts provide more accurate probabilities of victory and more information than debiased poll-based forecasts."* This is the most careful, apples-to-apples treatment and is the methodologically strongest paper in this literature — it essentially splits the difference with Erikson/Wlezien: markets win, but only once you've done the work Erikson/Wlezien insist on doing to the *polls* side too.
- **Favorite-longshot bias correction**: Wolfers, J., & Zitzewitz, E. (2006). "Interpreting Prediction Market Prices as Probabilities." NBER Working Paper 12200. https://www.nber.org/papers/w12200 (also at https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/wp06-11bk.pdf). Proposes a power-law correction, implied probability ≈ p^α with empirical α commonly cited around 0.7–0.8, i.e., raw market prices near the extremes should be pulled toward 50% before being treated as true probabilities. **This is a load-bearing, practically useful result for your team**: if you ever compare your model's output to raw Polymarket/Kalshi prices near the tails (e.g., a 90%+ market), apply a debiasing transform first, or you're comparing your calibrated probability to their miscalibrated one.

### 1c. Do markets overreact to narrative/vibes in a way models don't? (established, direct evidence)

- The 2022 episode above is itself evidence: the "red wave" was substantially a narrative/media construct (inflation, crime) that polling-fundamentals models resisted more than markets did.
- Direct quantitative evidence: Shriyash et al. (cited as a Vanderbilt-affiliated study) — **"Are Betting Markets Better than Polling in Predicting Political Elections?"**, arXiv:2507.08921, https://arxiv.org/html/2507.08921 (fetched directly) — fit Bayesian structural time series (BSTS) models to both Polymarket and polling-average data for 2024 and find explicitly: *"The Polymarket data exhibit more of a random-walk like time series characteristic of financial markets (i.e., has a higher degree of auto-regression)... Conversely, the polling data appear to be more characteristic of Gaussian noise distributed over a mean value."* They also document Polymarket reacting sharply to the Trump assassination attempt and to Harris entering the race, while "the polling data did not react similarly to these events and consistently hovered around a 45% chance of Trump winning." This is good, direct, peer-reviewed-adjacent (arXiv preprint) evidence that market prices have materially higher variance/autocorrelation — consistent with "reacting to vibes" — while poll aggregates are comparatively damped.
- Older, classic evidence on event-driven overreaction and mean reversion: Snowberg, E., Wolfers, J., & Zitzewitz, E., "Prediction Markets for Economic Forecasting," NBER Working Paper 18222 (2012), https://www.nber.org/papers/w18222, discusses markets' short-run reactions to debates/conventions and subsequent partial reversion — consistent with an overreaction-then-correction pattern, though I was only able to verify this at the level of the paper's existence and general thrust via search summary, not a direct quote from the text itself (**flag as partially verified**).

That is a reason to be cautious about generalizing "Polymarket was right in 2024" into "Polymarket will be right in 2026" — the mechanism was idiosyncratic. It also means Polymarket is demonstrably not perfectly liquid/efficient at the margin, which is directly relevant to your arbitrage model (thin liquidity + large traders = more exploitable mispricing, but also more risk that a "mispricing" you're fading is actually better-informed than you are).

### 1e. Broader forecasting-tournament evidence (established, general, not election-specific)
Atanasov, P.D., Witkowski, J., Mellers, B., & Tetlock, P.E. (2025). "Crowd prediction systems: Markets, polls, and elite forecasters." *International Journal of Forecasting*, 41(2), 580–595. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijforecast.2023.12.009 (also SSRN 4691513). Using a large multi-year forecasting tournament, markets and "prediction polls" (team/crowd forecasts, Good-Judgment-Project style) do **not differ significantly in overall accuracy**; small elite crowds beat larger ones; logarithmic-market-scoring-rule markets beat continuous double auctions on thin questions. This is general evidence (not election-specific) that "markets" are not inherently superior to well-run structured aggregation of informed judgment — relevant because your proposed model would effectively be competing as a structured alternative information-aggregation mechanism, and the literature says that's a fair fight, not a mismatch.

## 2. What actually drives midterm outcomes

### 2a. Established fundamentals and effect sizes

- **The midterm penalty is the single most robust finding in this literature.** The president's party has lost House seats in 20 of the last 22 midterms since 1938, with the only exceptions in highly unusual circumstances (2002: post-9/11 approval spike to 63%; 1998: backlash to Clinton impeachment attempt, 66% approval) — Galston, Brookings (2025), cited above, citing Gallup approval-tracker data.
- **Presidential approval** is the best-established single predictor and its correlation with seat losses "becomes tighter" as election day nears (Galston, Brookings 2025, citing presidency.ucsb.edu/analyses/the-2022-midterm-elections-what-the-historical-data-suggest). Historical average House loss ≈ 26–28 seats; Senate loss ≈ 3 seats for the president's party, though with considerable year-to-year variance (synthesized from multiple corroborating secondary sources I could not fully independently verify at the exact-seat-count level — **flag precision as approximate**).
- **"Surge and decline" / coattail-decay theory**: Campbell, A. et al., *The American Voter* (1960); Campbell, J.E., "The Presidential Surge and Its Midterm Decline in Congressional Elections, 1868–1988," *Journal of Politics* (1991), https://www.jstor.org/stable/2131768; Campbell, J.E., "The Presidential Pulse of Congressional Elections" (1997 book). Core mechanism: presidential-year "peripheral" voters who turn out for presidential coattails largely stay home at midterms, and the bigger the prior presidential win, the larger the subsequent midterm loss (FDR 1936 landslide → −72 House seats in 1938; LBJ 1964 landslide → −47 in 1966; Obama 2008 → −63 in 2010 — all cited directly in Galston/Brookings 2025).
- **The generic congressional ballot**, translated into seats via a two-step regression (national vote share → seats, separately modeling incumbent vs. open seats): **Bafumi, J., Erikson, R.S., & Wlezien, C. (2010). "Balancing, Generic Polls and Midterm Congressional Elections." *Journal of Politics*, 72(3), 705–719.** https://www.columbia.edu/~rse14/bafumi_erikson_wlezien_balancing.pdf (PDF fetched, text partially extractable). Companion: Bafumi, Erikson, & Wlezien (2010), "Forecasting House Seats from Generic Congressional Polls: The 2010 Midterm Election," *PS: Political Science & Politics*, 43(4), 633–636. Their "balancing" theory: as a midterm nears, poll preferences shift toward the out-party, consistent with a desire for divided government/policy moderation rather than pure disapproval. Achieved near-exact accuracy in 2006 (predicted 32-seat Democratic gain vs. actual 30). **Notably, this exact paper is independently cited as reference [3] and [4] in Decision Desk HQ's own live 2026 methodology** — confirming it remains the standard academic basis for generic-ballot-to-seats translation in production forecasting today.
- **Candidate quality / incumbency / money**: Jacobson, G.C. & Carson, J.L., *The Politics of Congressional Elections* (11th ed., 2024) — the standard textbook treatment. Key, durable findings: (a) high-quality challengers (prior elected experience) disproportionately enter races when they sense incumbent vulnerability — itself a signal correlated with the national environment; (b) incumbent campaign spending is a *lagging* indicator of trouble (incumbents spend more when in danger) while challenger spending is a leading indicator of a real threat; (c) the personal incumbency advantage has *declined* since the 1990s–2000s as straight-ticket, nationalized partisan voting has risen — Jacobson, "It's Nothing Personal: The Decline of the Incumbency Advantage in US House Elections," cited via JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1086/681670. This directly matters for a Senate model: in a more nationalized electorate, state-level "idiosyncratic" candidate-quality adjustments matter *less* than they used to, which argues for simpler, more nationally-driven models than a bespoke-candidate-quality model would suggest.

### 2b. Presidential-level analogues (useful as a template, not directly a midterm model)

- **Abramowitz, A. (1988, updated through subsequent cycles). "Time for Change" model** — predicts incumbent-party popular vote share from Q2 real GDP growth, mid-year presidential net approval, and a "time for change" penalty for parties holding the White House for 2+ terms. JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/420023; Cambridge PS piece on 2008 application. Accurate 1988–2012; **missed direction in 2016** (predicted a narrow GOP popular-vote win; Clinton won the popular vote, though wrong on the "mainstream candidate" assumption underlying the model).
- **Lewis-Beck, M.S. & Tien, C., "Political Economy Model"** — referendum-style model using presidential approval (Gallup, July) + GNP/GDP growth (first half of election year). Most recent applications: Lewis-Beck & Tien, "Election forecasting: Political economy models," *International Journal of Forecasting*, 41(4), 1655–1665 (2025), https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijforecast.2025.02.006; Tien & Lewis-Beck, "The Political Economy Model: Presidential Forecast for 2024," *PS: Political Science & Politics*, 58(2), 248–252 (2025).

## 3. Bayesian and simulation methods — where they genuinely add value

### 3a. The canonical lineage (established)

- **Gelman, A., & King, G. (1993). "Why Are American Presidential Election Campaign Polls So Variable When Votes Are So Predictable?" *British Journal of Political Science*, 23(4), 409–451.** The foundational insight motivating all subsequent Bayesian poll-aggregation work: vote outcomes are quite forecastable from fundamentals well before election day, so most of the poll-to-poll "noise" you see is sampling/house-effect variance around a comparatively stable latent trend — exactly the setup hierarchical pooling is designed to exploit.
- **Lock, K., & Gelman, A. (2010). "Bayesian Combination of State Polls and Election Forecasts." *Political Analysis*, 18(3), 337–348.** https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/D8WD4698. Forecasts the national vote and each state's relative position separately, partially pooling state polls, historical state lean, and economic/fundamentals priors.
- **Linzer, D.A. (2013). "Dynamic Bayesian Forecasting of Presidential Elections in the States." *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, 108(501), 124–134.** https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2012.737735; preprint https://votamatic.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/Linzer-JASA13.pdf. The direct ancestor of The Economist's model: borrows strength across states and time via a hierarchical dynamic linear model, filtering sampling noise from a latent daily opinion trend, and simulating the Electoral College from the posterior.
- **Heidemanns, M., Gelman, A., & Morris, G.E. (2020). "An Updated Dynamic Bayesian Forecasting Model for the US Presidential Election." *Harvard Data Science Review*, 2(4).** https://doi.org/10.1162/99608f92.fc62f1e1 — directly extends Linzer/Lock-Gelman with partisan non-response corrections, survey-mode adjustments, and empirically-estimated state correlations. This is literally the Economist model: confirmed via the model's own GitHub README, https://github.com/TheEconomist/us-potus-model (fetched directly): *"Improving on Pierre Kremp's implementation of Drew Linzer's dynamic linear model... we (1) add corrections for partisan non-response, survey mode and survey population; (2) use informative state-level priors that update throughout the election year; and (3) specify empirical state-level correlations from political and demographic variables."* The 2008 backtest achieved an (EV-weighted) Brier score of 0.032 and called 49/50 states correctly (per the repo's own posted performance table).

### 3b. MRP — what it buys you and what it costs (established)

Multilevel regression and poststratification (Gelman & Little (1997), "Poststratification into many categories using hierarchical logistic regression," *Survey Methodology*; canonical demonstration: Wang, W., Rothschild, D., Goel, S., & Gelman, A. (2015), "Forecasting elections with non-representative polls," the Xbox-survey study): fit a hierarchical regression of vote intention on demographics/geography, then reweight predicted cell probabilities by the true population frequency of each cell (typically Census/ACS). **What it buys you**: small-area estimates (e.g., state or district-level opinion) from a national or even badly-non-representative sample, and a principled way to "shrink" sparse-data cells toward reasonable priors instead of reporting noise. **What it needs that you may not have in two weeks**: individual-level microdata linking demographics to vote intention (not just topline polling), and a poststratification frame (Census/ACS or voter-file counts by cell) that matches your model's demographic breakdown. **Without microdata, MRP is not available to you** — you would be doing hierarchical partial-pooling across states/polls (Lock-Gelman/Linzer-style), which is a different and more tractable technique that only needs published topline poll numbers, not respondent-level data. This is an important practical distinction your team should internalize: "Bayesian hierarchical" and "MRP" are often used interchangeably in casual discussion but have very different data requirements — the former is feasible on public data in two weeks; the latter, for a credible state-level Senate model, generally is not without purchased or scraped microdata.

### 3c. How production models generate correlated national swing, and whether a one-factor copula is adequate (established mechanism; contested adequacy — this is a live methodological fault line, not settled)

**The mechanism, confirmed directly from two separate live model specifications:**
1. Decision Desk HQ 2026 (fetched directly, cited in full above, §0): single-factor probit copula, `y_i = μ(P_i) + λη + ε_i`, η ~ N(0,1) shared across all races, λ a scalar governing correlation strength.
2. The Economist/Gelman model: a full hierarchical Bayesian time-series with *state-specific* correlations derived empirically from demographic/political similarity between states (not literally one scalar factor, but conceptually a richer, multi-factor/structured covariance matrix) — per the GitHub README and the `state_correlation_matrix.csv` artifact in the repo, https://github.com/TheEconomist/us-potus-model/blob/master/data/state_correlation_matrix.csv.

**Is a one-factor copula adequate?** The literature's answer is: **it's a reasonable starting approximation, but getting the single scale/correlation parameter right is where "most of the work" is, and getting it wrong has caused two of the three most-discussed forecasting embarrassments of the last three cycles.** Direct evidence:

- Andrew Gelman's own blog, discussing a real, visually documented failure in FiveThirtyEight's (not the Economist's) **2020 presidential model**: "Problem of the between-state correlations in the Fivethirtyeight election forecast" (Aug 31, 2020) and the follow-up "Between-state correlations and weird conditional forecasts" (Oct 17, 2020) — both fetched via Wayback Machine since live fetch returned 403: https://web.archive.org/web/20200909161649/https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2020/08/31/problem-of-the-between-state-correlations-in-the-fivethirtyeight-election-forecast/. Gelman, quoting a colleague ("Elliott"): *"Seems like the arrows are pointing to a very weird covariance structure... How's Biden gonna win Idaho, Wyoming, Alabama... but not New Jersey?"* Gelman's diagnosis: *"this does all seem consistent with correlations of uncertainties between states that are too low... the between-state correlation is mostly not coming from the polling process at all; it's coming from uncertainty in public opinion changes among states. So you need some underlying statistical model of opinion swings in the 50 states, or else you need to hack in a correlation just right."* He directly quotes Nate Silver's own prior acknowledgment that correlated errors are *"where often most of the work is in modeling if you want your models to remotely resemble real-world conditions"* — i.e., even 538 knew this was the crux and still, in Gelman's assessment, got it wrong in live production in 2020.
- A second, **directly admitted** failure, this time in the Economist/Gelman model itself: Gelman, A. (2020, Oct 28). "Concerns with our Economist election forecast." https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2020/10/28/concerns-with-our-economist-election-forecast/ (cited via the HDSR 2024 piece's own reference list, confirmed directly). The Economist's 2020 model gave Biden ~96% to win the Electoral College vs. 538's ~89% and markets' ~60–66%. Gelman's own later retrospective (quoted via search synthesis, not independently fetched in full) concludes the model was **overconfident** — not wrong on the winner, but its implied uncertainty was too narrow given how correlated and potentially biased polling errors could be. The direct, load-bearing technical parameter at fault, per the Gelman/Economist team's own account in the HDSR 2024 piece (fetched directly): *"The most relevant parameter in our model is the standard deviation of the random walk of national vote preference over time... large enough variation over time could break this model and yield overconfident predictions."* — i.e., a single scalar (the innovation variance of the national-swing random walk) is explicitly identified by the model's own authors as the fulcrum on which overconfidence turns.
- **On your specific "Economist 2022 Senate model" question**: I found multiple AI-search-synthesized claims of a specific "correlation bug" in the 2022 Senate model that was later "fixed" by G. Elliott Morris, and separately, claims that the model exhibited large day-to-day swings in Senate-control probability that drew comparisons to a "martingale violation." **I was not able to independently verify either claim against a primary source** (G. Elliott Morris's Substack archive was not directly fetchable in this session, and the Economist's own 2022 Senate methodology page returned a 403). What I *can* verify directly is the general martingale-violation concern as a live, named concept in the forecasting literature: the Economist/Gelman team's own 2024 HDSR piece states explicitly, *"A probabilistic forecast should be a martingale... So a swing in predicted probability from 90% to 10%... should be very unlikely, and a forecasting procedure that regularly shows such swings has problems (Taleb, 2017)."* **I recommend treating the specific "2022 Economist Senate bug" story as plausible but unverified-by-me** — the *general phenomenon* (one-factor/correlation misspecification causing either implausible joint outcomes or excessive day-to-day volatility) is well documented for 2020 across two different models (538 and the Economist); whether the identical failure recurred in the Economist's 2022 Senate-specific model specifically, I could not confirm first-hand.
- An academic survey-style framing of the general critique, from search synthesis (not independently fetched in full — **secondary source**): a one-factor copula "imposes a uniform correlation structure... every pair of states moves together only based on a national component," which can understate region-specific correlation (e.g., Southern states moving together more than with Kansas), understate fat tails/rare joint events, and is typically "tuned" to match forecaster intuition about plausibility rather than estimated from a rigorous external criterion.

**Bottom line for your team**: a one-factor copula is not "wrong" in principle — it is literally what a live, major 2026 forecaster uses — but (a) its single correlation/variance parameter is doing enormous work and is usually chosen by judgment/backtesting rather than cleanly estimated, (b) getting it too low has caused visible, acknowledged failures (538 2020), and getting it too high (or the underlying random-walk variance too high) has caused acknowledged overconfidence-in-the-wrong-direction problems (Economist 2020). This is exactly the kind of thing your team's Monte Carlo/copula background is suited to stress-test — but the hard part is not implementing a Gaussian copula, it's *calibrating λ or the covariance matrix against history*, and the public record shows two of the best-resourced teams in this space (538, Economist/Gelman) have each visibly gotten it wrong once in the last three cycles.

### 3d. Monte Carlo vs. closed-form Poisson-Binomial (established, mostly a non-issue in practice)

If races were independent, the Poisson-Binomial distribution (exact via a known recursive/FFT algorithm — this is tractable for ~35 Senate races, contrary to one search result's claim of infeasibility; FFT-based Poisson-Binomial computation for n≈35–100 is fast and standard) gives an exact closed-form seat-count distribution with no simulation noise. **The reason virtually every real forecaster (538, Economist, DDHQ) uses Monte Carlo instead is specifically to inject the cross-race correlation structure discussed in §3c** — once you add a shared factor η, the race outcomes are no longer independent Bernoullis, and the closed-form Poisson-Binomial machinery (which assumes independence) no longer applies directly. **Practical implication for your team**: you do not need Monte Carlo for computational tractability (35 Senate races is nothing); you need it only because you want correlated draws. If you're willing to assume a single-factor Gaussian copula, there are actually semi-closed-form approaches (conditioning on η, computing a Poisson-Binomial given the conditional independent probabilities P(win_i | η), then integrating over η numerically) that would be faster and have zero simulation noise — worth considering given your team's quant background, though plain Monte Carlo (10,000–100,000 draws) is trivial at this scale and the added complexity may not be worth it.

## 4. Polling error — the thing that determines calibration

### 4a. The core empirical result (established, directly verified)

**Shirani-Mehr, H., Rothschild, D., Goel, S., & Gelman, A. (2018). "Disentangling Bias and Variance in Election Polls." *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, 113(522), 607–614.** https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2018.1448823; replication code at https://github.com/stanford-policylab/polling-errors; PDF at https://researchdmr.com/files/polling-errors.pdf (fetch attempted, binary PDF stream not text-extractable in this session — relying on consistent cross-source summary). Using 4,221 polls across 608 state-level presidential/Senate/gubernatorial elections, 1998–2014: **average total survey error (RMSE) ≈ 3.5 percentage points — roughly double what a standard reported margin of error would suggest.** They decompose this into (a) election-level bias (avg. absolute bias ≈ 1.5–2 points — i.e., *all* polls in a given race tend to share a common directional error, not cancel out) and (b) higher-than-advertised variance even after accounting for bias. **This is the single most important number for your calibration work**: whatever headline margin of error public pollsters report, treat the true standard error as roughly 2x that.

### 4b. Correlated errors across states — magnitude (established, with a useful quantified scenario-analysis)

**Isakov, M., & Kuriwaki, S. (2020). "Towards Principled Unskewing: Viewing 2020 Election Polls Through a Corrective Lens From 2016." *Harvard Data Science Review*, 2(4).** https://doi.org/10.1162/99608f92.86a46f38 (fetched directly via Wayback Machine, https://web.archive.org/web/20241207134157/https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/cnxbwum6/release/5). Applying Meng's (2018) "data defect index" framework to 12 battleground states, under the scenario that 2016-style nonresponse bias and turnout patterns persisted into 2020: *"our models shift Trump's estimated two-party voteshare by a percentage point in his favor in the median battleground state, and increases twofold the uncertainty around the voteshare estimate."* **The "twofold increase in uncertainty" figure is the quantification you asked for of how much correlated (vs. assumed-independent) state errors inflate true national-outcome uncertainty relative to naive aggregation.**

Separately, the Economist/Gelman team's own 2024 HDSR piece (cited above, §3c) states: *"A study of state-level polling errors since 2000 found a positive correlation among successive elections—that is, if state polls are biased toward the Republicans or Democrats one year, they are likely to have a similar bias in the next election"* — citing **Heidemanns, M. (2022), "Prediction and error: Forecast aggregation and adjustment," unpublished PhD thesis, Columbia University Department of Political Science.** This is an important, specific, and actionable claim: **state-level polling bias is serially correlated across cycles, not just within a cycle** — meaning your model should probably use 2022/2024 state-level polling-error direction/magnitude as an informative (not zero-mean) prior for 2026, not assume errors reset to zero mean each cycle. I was not able to independently fetch the underlying thesis to verify the exact correlation coefficient — **flag as a specific number I could not pin down, worth your team chasing the primary source if feasible.**

### 4c. 2016 and 2020 error magnitudes and partisan direction (established)

- **AAPOR Task Force (2021), "2020 Pre-Election Polling: An Evaluation of the 2020 General Election Polls,"** chaired by Josh Clinton (Vanderbilt), https://aapor.org/reports/2020-pre-election-polling-an-evaluation-of-the-2020-general-election-polls/. Key figures (via consistent cross-source synthesis; I was not able to extract clean text from the underlying PDF, which returned as a binary stream — **numbers below should be treated as well-corroborated but not independently page-verified by me**): national polls overstated Biden's margin by an average of ~3.9–4.5 points in the final two weeks; state-level polls averaged ~4.3 points of error; Senate/gubernatorial polls were worse, with the Democrat-Republican margin overstated by ~6 points on average and only ~66% of Senate polls correctly picking the winner. This was characterized as the worst performance for national polls in 40 years and for state polls in at least 20 years. The task force could not identify a single definitive cause, pointing to differential partisan nonresponse as the leading candidate explanation but explicitly declining to rule it conclusive.
- **AAPOR Task Force (2017), "An Evaluation of 2016 Election Polls in the United States,"** https://aapor.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/AAPOR-2016-Election-Polling-Report.pdf (PDF fetch attempted, binary stream). National polls were historically *accurate* in 2016 (~3-point Clinton lead vs. ~2-point actual popular-vote win); the failure was concentrated in a cluster of Midwestern battleground states, substantially attributed in post-hoc analysis to under-weighting non-college-educated white voters.

### 4d. Is the partisan direction of polling error predictable ex ante? **This is the contested, practically crucial question, and the honest answer is: not reliably, in real time, before the fact.**

- 2016 and 2020 both saw polls err in the *same* direction (understating the Republican/Trump vote) — tempting to call this a predictable pattern. But: (a) 2012 and 2022 did not show the same clear pro-Democratic bias (2022 polls, if anything, were roughly balanced to slightly favoring Republicans, consistent with the "red wave" overprediction discussed in §1); (b) the AAPOR 2020 task force explicitly could not identify a mechanism that would let you confidently predict the *sign* of the next cycle's error in advance, only that nonresponse bias is a plausible ongoing structural risk. The Heidemanns (2022) finding of serial correlation in state-level bias direction (§4b) is the strongest evidence of ex-ante predictability I found, but it comes from a single unpublished thesis I could not independently verify in detail. **Net assessment: treat "the polls are probably biased toward Democrats again" as a weakly-informed prior worth a small adjustment, not a confident, back-tested trading signal.**

## 5. Practical verdict

### Could a careful small team build a better-calibrated Senate-control model than Polymarket in under two weeks, using only public data? **Honestly, no — and the literature itself is the reason why, not a lack of effort.**

Here is why, directly from the evidence above:
1. **The hardest part of a good model (getting cross-race correlation right) is exactly the part where two of the best-funded, most methodologically sophisticated teams in this space (538 in 2020, and by secondary-sourced but unverified account, the Economist in 2022) have visibly gotten it wrong in the last three cycles** (§3c). This is not a data-engineering problem you can brute-force in two weeks; it's a parameter that the literature says is usually "hacked in" via judgment and backtesting even by experts, and errors in either direction (too low → implausible joint scenarios; too high on the random-walk variance → overconfidence) have both happened.
2. **Polling error itself is ~2x the naive margin of error and non-trivially correlated across states and across cycles** (§4a–4b) — meaning your calibration will be dominated by a nuisance parameter (true state-error covariance) that the best-resourced academic/journalistic teams have spent over a decade refining and still don't have fully nailed down (the Economist/Gelman team's own 2024 paper is, after all, a list of open uncertainties, not a solved problem).
3. **Decision Desk HQ's model — arguably the most "beatable-looking" of the named models at 55-56% — already incorporates Polymarket and Kalshi prices directly as a weighted input layer** (§0). This means you are not really racing to beat an independent competitor; part of what you'd be trying to beat has partially already priced in the market you're comparing it to. A genuinely independent, non-market-informed fundamentals+polling model (more like the Economist's or Silver's) is the fairer comparison, and those sit at 57% and 65% respectively — i.e., even the serious independent models don't agree with each other within several points, which should calibrate your expectations about how tight two weeks of public-data work could plausibly get you.
4. **The historical scorecard (§1b) does not support assuming the market is usually wrong** — it's roughly a coin flip across the last four cycles, with the market's one clear win (2024) partly attributable to an idiosyncratic large trader with privately-commissioned polling, not generic market efficiency (§1d).

### If not clearly better, could it be good enough to identify when the market is likely wrong? **Yes — this is the right-sized, honest target, and it's achievable.**

This reframes the problem from "build a superior oracle" to "build a well-calibrated null hypothesis with honest uncertainty, and flag divergence as a trading signal, not a certainty." This is close to what the Sethi et al. (2021) paper (§1b) actually recommends and formalizes: their finding that **a simple average of model and market outperforms either alone**, and that the model and market "make different kinds of errors in different states," is directly actionable — build the model not to replace the market comparison you already do, but to produce a second, methodologically independent prior that you weight against the market price, flagging large divergences (like the current ~10 points) as situations warranting *increased* position sizing on the "fade the market" side only when your fundamentals/polling-based prior and the market disagree by more than historical model-vs-model disagreement would predict (i.e., is a 10-point gap unusual relative to the typical 538-vs-Economist-vs-DDHQ spread, which itself routinely runs 5–10 points per your own data above? If the gap you're puzzling over is within the normal range of disagreement *between serious models themselves*, that's informative — it says the anomaly may be less special than it looks).

### Minimum viable version and data requirements

A defensible two-week build, grounded in the literature above, would be:
1. **Generic-ballot-to-seats layer**: replicate Bafumi/Erikson/Wlezien (2010) — publicly available generic-ballot polling averages (DDHQ, 538/ABC, RealClearPolitics all publish these) regressed against historical national-vote-to-seat translations. Needs: historical generic ballot + seat outcomes by cycle (public, scrapeable).
2. **Fundamentals layer**: presidential approval (Gallup/aggregator, public), a midterm-penalty/president's-party dummy, state partisan lean (prior presidential margins, public), incumbency flags (public). This replicates the structural core of DDHQ's own published fundamentals ensemble (§0) — you now have their exact feature list to benchmark against.
3. **State-level polling aggregation layer**: partial-pooling hierarchical model à la Lock & Gelman (2010) / Linzer (2013) on public state polls (538's and DDHQ's own poll databases are public or scrapeable), explicitly widening reported margins by the Shirani-Mehr ~2x factor (§4a), and explicitly correlating state errors via a single-factor copula (λ chosen by backtesting against 2018/2020/2022/2024 state-level errors) — this is exactly your team's stated strength (Bayesian hierarchical + copula + Monte Carlo), and it is squarely where the literature says real value is added, provided you backtest λ rather than guess it.
4. **Explicit, stated uncertainty on λ/correlation itself** — given that this is the single parameter that has visibly broken two major models in three cycles, your team should treat it as a stress-tested range (e.g., report outcomes at λ_low/λ_mid/λ_high) rather than a point estimate, and should prefer being visibly more humble (wider bands) than overconfident, per the Economist's own admitted 2020 lesson.
5. **Do not attempt MRP** without purchasing or scraping microdata (voter file + a demographically-coded poll/panel) — it is not a two-week, public-data-only technique for a credible state-level Senate build (§3b).
6. **Use the market price as an input, not a target to beat** — construct your model independently, then treat the gap between your model and the market as the tradable signal itself, in the spirit of Sethi et al. (2021)'s model+market averaging result, rather than trying to make your model stand alone as a superior oracle.
