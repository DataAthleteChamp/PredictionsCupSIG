## 1. Favourite-Longshot Bias (FLB)

**Canonical literature — bibliography and effect sizes.** Griffith (1949, *Am. J. Psychology* 62(2):290-294), McGlothlin (1956, *Am. J. Psychology* 69(4):604-615, DOI 10.2307/1419083), Weitzman (1965, *JPE* 73(1):18-26), Ali (1977, *JPE* 85(4):803-815, DOI 10.1086/260600), Thaler & Ziemba (1988, *JEP* 2(2):161-174, DOI 10.1257/jep.2.2.161 — https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.2.2.161), Jullien & Salanié (2000, *JPE* 108(3):503-530) are all genuine, bibliographically confirmed **[peer-reviewed]**. The cleanest modern effect-size replication is **Snowberg & Wolfers (2010)**, who overlay the full 1949-2004 lineage on 5.6-6.4M US/Australian/UK starts (1992-2001+) and find the shape essentially unchanged since Griffith: return on odds ≥100/1 ≈ **-61%**, random betting ≈ **-23%**, betting the favorite every race ≈ **-5.5%**, mid-range (4/1-9/1) flat ≈ **-18%** (NBER WP15923, directly fetched — https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15923/w15923.pdf). They explicitly retested and **overturned** two older claims: (a) that longshot bias worsens in the day's last race (loss-chasing) — "no longer evident in recent data"; (b) Thaler-Ziemba's claimed positive return to extreme favorites — "not true in any of our datasets."

**"Page & Clarke" does not exist as a paper** — almost certainly a misremembering of **Page, L. & Clemen, R.T. (2013), "Do Prediction Markets Produce Well-Calibrated Probability Forecasts?"**, *Economic Journal* 123:491-513, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0297.2012.02561.x **[peer-reviewed]** (full PDF fetched: people.duke.edu/~clemen/bio/Published%20Papers/45.PredictionMarkets-Page&Clemen-EJ-2013.pdf) — see §4. A related solo paper is Page (2012), "'It ain't over till it's over': Yogi Berra bias on prediction markets," *Applied Economics* 44(1):81-92 — InTrade sports markets, calibration deteriorates sharply in a game's **final minutes** specifically.

**THE critical question — risk-love vs. misperception.** **Snowberg & Wolfers (2010), *JPE* 118(4):723-746**, DOI 10.1086/655844 (free NBER preprint above, fetched and read in full) resolve this by using compound/exotic bets (exacta/quinella/trifecta) to break the observational-equivalence between the two theories on simple win bets. Verbatim conclusion: *"We find evidence in favor of the view that misperceptions of probability drive the favorite-longshot bias, as suggested by Prospect Theory."* **This matters directly for your question of persistence:** their own explanation for *why the bias survives in equilibrium* is explicit — *"This bias likely persists in equilibrium because misperceptions are not large enough to generate profit opportunities for unbiased bettors."* I.e., it is framed as a **stable, structural feature of the price curve**, not a disequilibrium that should mechanically arbitrage away as a market matures or thins. The paper itself does not study time-to-resolution dynamics — that is a distinct body of work (§4).

**IEM is the one clean counter-example.** Berg & Rietz, "Longshots, Overconfidence and Efficiency on the Iowa Electronic Market," *Intl. J. Forecasting* 35(1):271-287 (2019) — directly fetched (https://www.biz.uiowa.edu/faculty/trietz/papers/Longshots.pdf): *"No longshot bias appears in IEM markets."* IEM is real-money but non-profit, $500-position-capped, academic. They do find a *different*, opposite-direction "overconfidence" bias (favorites underperform slightly, longshots overperform slightly) concentrated at intermediate horizons that **"disappears as the event approaches."**

**Does it strengthen or decay near resolution? Mixed by regime — do not apply a universal law:**
- **Continuous in-play markets (sports clock):** bias **strengthens** late — Page's "Yogi Berra effect" and Whelan (2025/26), "Agreeing to Disagree: The Economics of Betting Exchanges" (Betfair soccer, 200k+ matches): Taker longshot losses grow through the match, reaching ~70% loss rates by ~105 minutes; Maker losses on longshots are "almost as bad" late, even though Makers break even or profit earlier.
- **Episodic forecast markets (elections — your regime):** bias/calibration **improves** approaching resolution. This is the better-supported pattern for your use case — see §4 for the full evidence chain (Page & Clemen, Rothschild 2009, Leigh & Wolfers 2006, Berg et al., Bürgi-Deng-Whelan all point the same direction).
- **Net profitability after costs:** no source finds a large, robust, easily-harvested profit from a naive "always fade longshots" rule on a normal (unbiased) venue — Levitt (2004, *Economic Journal* 114(495):223-246, cited via Snowberg-Wolfers' own reference list) argues bookmakers deliberately shade odds to **capture** the bias as house margin (~5.0%→6.16%), not leave it as free money. The modest exceptions with documented *net*-positive returns are narrow: Kalshi contracts ≥50¢ (small positive even for Takers), and Betfair favorites specifically in the second half of live matches (Whelan). **This is why your situation is structurally different and more interesting**: you're not fading a bookmaker's shaded book, you're arbing a venue against an external index (Polymarket) that is measurably mispriced *relative to that index* — a different, and less-studied, setup than anything in this literature.

## 2. Convergence and Cross-Venue Arbitrage

**Price discovery, 2024 election.** Ng, Peng, Tao & Zhou, "Price Discovery and Trading in Modern Prediction Markets" (SSRN 5331995 **[working paper]**; verified via author's own conference slides, hunterng.com/files/ff-slides_v20260905.pdf, directly fetched): over Oct 4-Nov 5 2024, Polymarket priced Trump **2.6-3.2¢ higher on average** than Kalshi/PredictIt (persistent, significant at 1%); VAR lead-lag shows **Polymarket→Kalshi significant (0.198*** for Trump contracts), reverse direction insignificant**, with an impulse response half-life of **~3 hours**. Important nuance from the same paper's full (non-election) 2.5-year, 1,972-matched-pair sample: **leadership reverses by category** — Kalshi leads more on a volume-weighted basis overall and specifically leads **sports**; Polymarket leads specifically in **politics**, and that political lead is roughly **doubled** in categories an LLM-based classifier scores as higher "insider-information-risk" — i.e., part of Polymarket's political lead is statistically tied to elevated insider-risk categories, not pure superior public processing. A hypothetical Kalshi-Polymarket arbitrage (5-min bars) would have grossed $2.58M / netted ~$1.0M after a 2% cost haircut over the sample — explicitly flagged by the authors as **not legally capturable** (geoblocking).

**How fast did gaps close? Slower than you'd think.** Clinton & Huang, "Prediction Markets? The Accuracy and Efficiency of Political Prediction Markets in the 2024 Presidential Election" (OSF/SocArXiv d5yx2, **[working paper]**, full text directly fetched — ~2,200 markets, $3.2B volume, final nine weeks): same-day price-*change* correlation across venues is only **0.6-0.7**; full alignment requires cumulating **~a week** of lag (rising to 0.91 Polymarket-Kalshi, 0.77-0.74 vs PredictIt). Their own words: *"There is no evidence of systemic and persistent mispricing, but similar markets are not immediately incorporating the same information in identical ways."* Most strikingly for your "does the gap close as the deadline nears" question: a costless cross-venue arbitrage (cheapest Trump+Harris combo, after real fee schedules) existed on **nearly every day Sept 1-Nov 4**, **grew in the final two weeks, peaked in the final days, and converged to zero only once vote-counting began on election night** — the opposite of simple EMH convergence-as-deadline-nears. *(Caution: an earlier search-summary pass attributed different, wrong headline numbers — PredictIt 93%/Kalshi 78%/Polymarket 67% accuracy — to this same paper; having read the actual text, real figures are Kalshi 89.8%, Polymarket 85.3%, PredictIt 81.8% (N=33 only), with the Polymarket/Kalshi gap attributed mostly to Polymarket's heavy "speech contract" mix, not inherent inaccuracy. This is flagged explicitly as a caught AI-search fabrication — treat any pre-verification figures on this topic with real suspicion.)*

## 3. Market Microstructure of Binary Event Contracts

**Pre-2024 academic base.** Oliven & Rietz (2004), *Management Science* 50(3):336-351 **[peer-reviewed]**, IEM: marginal (price-setting) trader rationality, not average-trader rationality, drives aggregate efficiency; tighter spreads correlate with fewer efficiency violations. Restocchi, McGroarty & Gerding (2019), *Physica A* 515:159-170 **[peer-reviewed]** — note: this is a **PredictIt**, not Betfair, dataset (3,385 political markets) — finds volatility clustering, long memory, fat tails, power-law volume, "characteristics similar to emerging markets." Croxson & Reade (2014), *Economic Journal* 124(575):62-91 **[peer-reviewed]**, 1,200+ Betfair in-play football matches: prices adjust "swiftly and fully" to goals, no half-time drift — a speed/completeness-of-adjustment finding, not directly a spread/adverse-selection study.

**The 2025-2026 wave (all working papers — zero peer-reviewed-published Kalshi/Polymarket microstructure papers exist yet as of Oct 2026):**
- **Bartlett & O'Hara (2026)**, Stanford Rock Center/Olin WP, SSRN 6615739 (abstract confirmed via direct fetch of law.stanford.edu) — O'Hara co-developed the PIN/VPIN toxicity framework. 41.6M Kalshi trades: single-name markets show **greater informed price impact** (Kyle's λ) than broad-based markets, yet **market makers earn ~2x more per contract** there — resolved by a *behavioral* surplus (traders systematically overbet YES in markets that mostly settle NO) that cross-subsidizes the adverse selection. VPIN-style toxicity predicts maker losses in single-name markets specifically, not broad-based ones.
- **Dubach (2026)**, arXiv:2604.24366 (confirmed real via direct fetch), under review at *J. Financial Markets*: 30.3B Polymarket order-book events. Two findings matter most for you: (1) **public-feed trade-direction inference is only ~59% accurate** vs. ground-truth on-chain data (vs. ~80% for Lee-Ready on Nasdaq) — any toxicity signal you build off the public WebSocket feed alone is likely to be noisy/wrong; (2) quoted half-spread is ~200bps at the 0.4-0.6 mid-price decile but **650-900bps below the 0.10 decile** — an order of magnitude wider than equities. The author's own reading explicitly pushes back on a pure adverse-selection/misperception story: *"That scale looks less like risk-love or misperception and more like a constraint on liquidity provision"* (capital/inventory cost).
- **Bürgi, Deng & Whelan** (same CEPR DP20631 as §1): Makers earn more than Takers across the board, gap largest on longshots.

**"Pinning" near expiry — the options analogy does NOT transfer, and the evidence points the opposite way.** There is no dealer delta-hedging mechanism underlying a prediction-market contract (no underlying asset to hedge), so the mechanical cause of options pinning (Avellaneda-Lipkin-style dealer gamma flows) has **no structural analogue** here — treat the pinning intuition as unsupported by any source, not merely untested. Separately, as a matter of basic probability: the **unconditional** variance of a bounded martingale heading to a terminal 0/1 outcome is *non-decreasing* over calendar time and is maximized exactly at resolution (the opposite of "things calm down"); what can shrink is the *conditional* residual uncertainty given information revealed so far, and that is governed by the **rate of information arrival**, not the calendar clock — it can even *rise* in a contested race. The one direct empirical liquidity measure found, Dubach's cross-sectional order-book-depth regression, points toward **thinner, not deeper, books** closer to resolution (depth falls ~6% per 10x reduction in seconds-to-close, controlling for volume) — weak evidence *against* a calming "pinning" effect.

**Informed-trading/adverse-selection risk is real, documented, and appears to concentrate around resolving information — this is the more important operational finding than pinning.** Mitts & Ofir, "From Iran to Taylor Swift: Informed Trading in Prediction Markets" (SSRN 6426778; verified in full via the Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance post, directly fetched): systematic screen of **all** Polymarket markets Feb 2024-Feb 2026 (93,000+ markets, ~50,000 wallets) flags **210,718 suspicious wallet-market pairs** with a **69.9% win rate** (>60 standard deviations above chance), **~$143M aggregate anomalous profit** (likely a lower bound — buy-side only, excludes sub-$500 positions). Concrete named cases: a wallet trading 71 minutes before the Feb 2026 US-Israeli Iran strike ($553k profit); a wallet turning $38.5k into $485k ahead of the Maduro-capture announcement; an Israeli civilian and IDF reservist indicted for trading classified intel on Polymarket; informed-looking trades ahead of Google's "Year in Search" and OpenAI's browser launch and Taylor Swift's engagement announcement. Directly relevant to **political** contracts: Reuters (Douglas Gillison, ~June 2026, directly fetched) reports Kalshi suspended three congressional candidates for betting on their own races, George Santos under investigation for Kalshi insider trading, and the **U.S. Senate banned members/staff from prediction-market betting (April 2026)**. A UK precedent is directly on point: a Conservative aide and an aide's associate **pleaded guilty** (June 2026) to betting on the 2024 UK election *date* using non-public government information — proof that informed trading on a scheduled political-resolution event is a real, prosecuted risk category, not a theoretical one.

**Market-maker profitability net of adverse selection — positive on average, but degrades specifically on longshots near resolution.** Whelan (2025/26) Betfair: pre-match/early in-play, Makers generally break even or outperform; **"longshot bets lose an increasing amount of money as the game nears its end, even for Makers."** This is the single cleanest quantified answer to "how does MM profitability vary with both time-to-resolution and price extremity simultaneously" — and it says the danger zone is specifically **longshots, specifically late**. **No study isolates election-contract-specific spread/toxicity behavior in the final days before a *scheduled* (non-sports-clock) resolution date** — this is a genuine, explicitly-flagged gap across all three independent research passes, and it's precisely your operational situation.

## 4. Election-Specific Timing Effects

**Core, multi-source, directly-verified finding: calibration/accuracy improves (not worsens) approaching a scheduled election, in contrast to the in-play sports regime.**
- **Page & Clemen (2013)**, *Economic Journal* **[peer-reviewed]**, full text fetched: explicit time buckets (≤1 day / ≤10 days / 11-100 days / >100 days) on InTrade data — *"prediction markets are reasonably well calibrated when time to expiration is relatively short, but prices are significantly biased for events farther in the future."* **Important caveat specific to your contracts**: political markets showed a *stronger* longshot bias than their pooled (non-political) sample even close to resolution — a 20¢ political contract resolved true only 10.9% of the time vs. 15.3% pooled; an 80¢ one resolved 92.8% vs. 87.4% pooled.
- **Rothschild (2009)**, *Public Opinion Quarterly* 73(5):895-916 **[peer-reviewed]**, full text fetched, 74 races/2008 cycle: debiased-market advantage over polls is *strongest early in the cycle* (probit coefficient 1.407 "before Labor Day" vs. 1.153 for near-certain late-cycle races), because polls take days to saturate while markets price news immediately — implying the market/poll/outcome gap **narrows**, not widens, into the close.
- **Leigh & Wolfers (2006)**, *Economic Record* 82(258):325-340 / NBER WP12053 **[peer-reviewed]**, fetched directly — note this is the **Australian 2004 election**, not a US one: seat-market accuracy was "substantially poorer" three weeks out than on election eve.
- **Berg, Forsythe, Nelson & Rietz**, IEM, directly fetched: election-eve market error 1.49% vs. final-week-average 1.58% vs. poll error 1.91% — accuracy keeps improving to the close; markets with more volume near the election perform better.
- **Bürgi, Deng & Whelan** (CEPR DP20631, §1): Kalshi prices directly confirmed (verbatim from the abstract I fetched myself) to *"become more accurate as markets approach closing."*
- **A genuine, flagged contradiction for current platforms**: an independent (non-peer-reviewed) 2025-26 blog analysis of recent Polymarket data (github.com/GitHubMagnus/polymarket-calibration, 3,018 resolved markets) found calibration error falling from 5.1pp (30 days out) to 1.6pp (1 day out) — consistent with the above — **but also found no classic favourite-longshot bias at all in this recent sample**, directly contradicting the Cardozo arXiv paper's finding of strong politics-category FLB. **Whether current-generation Polymarket exhibits FLB at all is a live, contested question between two different samples/methods — do not treat it as settled.** The same blog analysis found a *separate* "distance mispricing": 50-70%-priced contracts resolved YES 84% of the time measured 30 days out vs. 69% at 7 days out, vanishing by 1 day out.
- **No study anywhere measures bid-ask spread as a direct function of days-to-election.** Confirmed absent after extensive searching — a real gap, not an oversight.

**Intraday reaction to news — fast, large, but gated by how anticipated the news was (not by volume).** Snowberg, Wolfers & Zitzewitz (2007), *QJE* 122(2):807-829 **[peer-reviewed]**, full text fetched: Election Night 2004 exit-poll leak moved Bush's Tradesports win probability **-12pp in one hour** (3-4pm), correction brought it back to 95%, with correlated S&P500/Treasury moves of similar timing (interestingly, "political news was reflected in the stock market slightly before the prediction market" in this instance); election-day bid-ask spread was a tight 0.5% of expiry value. Wolfers & Zitzewitz (2016 Brookings piece): first 2016 debate moved Clinton's Betfair price 63¢→69¢ over a ~2-hour window, matched by S&P futures/Treasury/FTSE moves. **Most actionable: Tsang & Yang (2026), arXiv:2603.03152** (confirmed real), three 2024 shocks at 30-min/3-hr horizons — the **debate's price move largely reverses within 3 hours**; the **assassination attempt's move persists and grows** (+5¢/+10¢); **Biden's withdrawal generated the heaviest volume of the three events with almost no change in the Trump price**, because the market had already priced ~70% odds of withdrawal. Their explicit, quotable conclusion: *"A standard regularity in asset markets links trading volume to the size of price changes... Biden's withdrawal breaks that link."* **Operationally: do not use volume spikes as a proxy for repricing magnitude — the size of a move is governed by how anticipated the news was, not by how much gets traded on it.**

**Closing-line value (CLV) — confirmed negative finding.** Despite extensive multi-angle searching, **no peer-reviewed or working-paper-level study defines or tests CLV in political/election markets** — every substantive CLV discussion found is sports-betting-specific and practitioner/blog-tier (thelines.com, evbets.app, and similar). The closest adjacent academic concept — Gómez-Cram et al., "Prediction Market Accuracy: Crowd Wisdom or Informed Minority?" (reportedly ~3% of Polymarket accounts are "persistently skilled," 44-66% out-of-sample persistence, skilled net buying predicts convergence toward the resolution price) — **could not be independently verified at the primary-source level** (SSRN blocked). **If you build a CLV-style metric for this asset class, you'd be doing novel, unvalidated work, not applying an established technique.**

## 5. Chamber-Control vs. Seat-Level Consistency

**Explicit negative finding: nobody has published this specific test.** Despite targeted searches of academic sources and the most likely quant/economist commentators (Rajiv Sethi's Substack and Andrew Gelman's blog, where Sethi guest-posts on exactly this market), **no one could be found who tested whether an aggregate "Senate/House control" market priced consistently with what's implied by combining the individual seat/race markets**, for 2024 or any cycle. This is a genuine open gap, not a settled "no" — it might be a real exploitable niche, or it might be hard for good reasons (thin liquidity in down-ballot contracts, no combinatorial order book to force consistency). The closest related work: Sethi et al. (2025), *Proc. ACM Collective Intelligence Conf.*, pp. 214-225 (abstract verified via Gelman's blog quoting it directly) compares forecaster *models* (538, Economist, Silver Bulletin) against Polymarket *prices* race-by-race — a different question (model-vs-market accuracy, not aggregate-vs-component internal consistency) — finding *"all models failed to beat the market in the headline contract but some did so convincingly in... less visible races."* Separately, Sethi noted Polymarket and PredictIt prices for the *same* presidential contract diverged up to 2x and were "not easy to arbitrage... for logistical reasons" — cross-platform, not aggregate-vs-component, but a relevant adjacent data point. **Theoretical grounding for why inconsistency is even possible**: Hanson (2003), "Combinatorial Information Market Design," *Information Systems Frontiers* 5(1):107-119 — standalone markets for an aggregate outcome and its constituent events are not automatically arbitrage-consistent absent a unified combinatorial market design.

**How reputable forecasters actually model correlation — uniformly richer than one-factor, verified from primary sources/code:**
- **Linzer (2013)**, *JASA* 108(501):124-134 **[peer-reviewed]**, original PDF fetched: a genuine **2-component** structure — state-level effect + **one single national shock applied identically to every state** — the closest real precedent to "one-factor," but only as 2013 academic precedent, not a model anyone fields today.
- **The Economist's `us-potus-model`** (GitHub, Stan code fetched directly — `scripts/model/poll_model_2020.stan`): a full **empirically-estimated S×S state covariance matrix**, built from 9 demographic/political similarity variables (2016 vote, race, education, age, density, evangelical share), applied via Cholesky decomposition at **three separate layers** (daily random-walk innovations, final-day prior, and a persistent non-decaying "polling bias" term). A **Student-t(4) fat-tailed alternative is explicitly coded but commented out** (Normal ships in production); development artifacts show they tested a ~0.6 correlation parameter and the Student-t variant. This is genuinely multi-factor, not one-factor.
- **538/ABC News 2024** (methodology articles fetched directly): **Senate** — explicit 3-tier decomposition with published numbers: **4.8 points national error, 2.8 regional, 4.8 state-idiosyncratic**, plus a separate always-correlated "black swan" overlay (~1 point, hits every race simultaneously). **House** — explicit **5-tier** decomposition: **3 national / 2 regional / 2 demographic-cluster / 2 state / 6 district-idiosyncratic**, plus a correlated overlay (~2 points). The demographic-cluster tier (grouping non-adjacent districts sharing demographics) directly parallels the Economist's approach.
- **Split Ticket**: strong real-world 2024 record (97.7% of 525 federal races called correctly) but **no disclosed correlation methodology found** despite searching. **Decision Desk HQ**: primary methodology text blocked (geo-block/JS-SPA/403) — any DDHQ claims are unverified. **"RMG Analytics" could not be confirmed to exist** as an independent modeler — likely conflated with RMG Research's "Scott's Scoreboard," which is a meta-*aggregator* of other forecasts/prices, not its own model.

**A real, documented case study of correlated-tail-risk failure — directly relevant to your one-factor-adequacy question.** The Economist's own 2022 Senate/House model (archived methodology article, fetched): *"Between June 1st and October 16th, we overestimated the probability that the Democrats would hold the House... by an average of 7.4 percentage points. For the chance of Democrats keeping the Senate, we overstated... by 6.1 points."* Root cause: two new pollster firms released correlated pro-Democratic polls across nearly every competitive Senate race simultaneously, and the model's house-effect correction (which relies on each pollster's own track record) had no prior data on brand-new firms, so its assumption that "new pollsters as a group would not tend to favour one side" failed — producing a **simultaneous, correlated miss across both chambers at once**. (The CDO/Li-copula framing — that thin-tailed one-factor models systematically underestimate joint tail risk — is **my inference/analogy supplied for your context, not a claim any forecaster makes about themselves**; but the Economist's 2022 episode is a directly-documented real instance of exactly that failure mode.)

**Bottom line on one-factor adequacy**: every fielded 2024-era model uses multi-factor/hierarchical structures with published non-trivial numbers at each tier; none uses a literal flat one-factor Gaussian copula. A naive one-factor model is likely to be **systematically overconfident about joint ("wave") scenarios** — exactly the Economist's 2022 experience — which cuts both ways for you: it's a reason a chamber-control-vs-seats arbitrage might be real (if the market itself prices correlation naively), but also a reason your own detection model could generate false "arbitrage" signals if *your* correlation assumption is the naive one.

## 6. What Actually Works — Ranked by Evidential Strength

**Tier 1 — strongest support, most directly actionable:**
1. **Fading extreme longshots (<10-15¢) against a credible fair-value anchor.** The single most replicated finding in this literature across 75+ years and nearly every venue (racetracks, Betfair, Kalshi, Polymarket; IEM is the sole clean exception). Your own venue-specific measurement (1.95-2.79×, p=3e-08) sits consistently with documented magnitudes (Kalshi sub-10¢ loses >60%; Polymarket politics longshots -46% dollar-weighted). **Caveat 1**: aggregation unit matters enormously — Cardozo et al. show the sign can flip between parent-event and child-market weighting, so validate which unit your edge is measured in. **Caveat 2**: the classic literature's own verdict is that this is *not* free money net of normal costs on an unbiased venue (Snowberg-Wolfers, Levitt) — your edge is credible specifically *because* your venue is independently measured as mispriced vs. an external anchor, which is a different and less-studied setup than "beat the bookmaker's vig."
2. **Treat Polymarket as a slightly-biased anchor, not a perfect one, at the tails.** Two independent calibration datasets agree Polymarket itself overprices low-probability political contracts (politics 82.5%-bucket resolved at 73.97%; 10-20¢ contracts resolved true only 7.1% of the time). This likely means your 1.95-2.79× figure understates true mispricing at the extremes — a reason for, not against, the longshot-fade thesis, but also a reason not to assume "matches Polymarket" equals "fair value" below ~5% or above ~95%.

**Tier 2 — real support, needs care in implementation:**
3. **Passive market-making is net-profitable on average in this asset class** (Betfair, Kalshi evidence), consistent with your measured grind — but profitability **concentrates on the Maker/favorite side and degrades specifically on longshots as resolution nears** (Whelan's Betfair finding: longshot losses grow "even for Makers" late). Order-book depth evidence (Dubach) shows books get *thinner*, not calmer, near resolution — combine this with the documented, real, and growing insider/informed-trading risk around political contracts specifically (Mitts-Ofir's $143M finding; Santos investigation; Senate staff ban) to size down or widen longshot-side quotes in the final 1-2 weeks rather than assuming expiry brings calm.
4. **Chamber-control-vs-seat-market consistency checks are a genuinely unexploited niche** (nobody has published this test) — worth building, but only with an explicitly multi-factor/fat-tailed correlation model (538/Economist-style, not flat one-factor), sized conservatively, since a naive correlation assumption is a documented real failure mode (Economist 2022) and any detected "inconsistency" is as likely to be your model's correlation error as the market's mispricing.

**Tier 3 — documented to fail, or to look attractive and be unvalidated — do not treat as known edges:**
- **Naive "always back the favorite"/"always lay the longshot" on an unbiased venue**: explicitly tested and found unprofitable net of costs (Snowberg-Wolfers; Levitt's bookmaker-capture explanation).
- **Loss-chasing/last-race-of-day intensification of FLB**: a classic finding (McGlothlin/Ali) explicitly retested on modern data and overturned — "no longer evident."
- **"Fade the whale" / fade large directional flow**: not validated in either direction by any source; the one concrete 2024 case (Théo) shows the big bettor was right, wasn't actually a single dominant actor (15% of flow, two-sided throughout), and "vindication" rests on a sample of one plus self-interested reporting (Polymarket's own CEO). Treat large directional flow as potentially informed (cf. the extensive 2024-2026 insider-trading evidence), not as noise to automatically fade.
- **Using volume spikes as a proxy for incoming repricing magnitude**: directly falsified by the Biden-withdrawal case (heaviest volume of three 2024 shock events, ~zero price change, because it was already anticipated).
- **Mechanical "pinning" / expecting risk to calm into expiry**: unsupported — no structural mechanism transfers from options, and the limited direct evidence (thinner books, elevated informed-trading incidence) points the opposite way.
- **Closing-line-value strategies transplanted from sports betting**: zero rigorous political-market literature exists; this would be unvalidated experimentation, not a known technique.
- **Treating either exchange's self-published calibration research as independent validation**: both are self-reported by the venue being evaluated; use the independent sources (Themis/calibration.city, Clinton-Huang) instead, with appropriate N-size caution in the thin high-confidence political bins.
