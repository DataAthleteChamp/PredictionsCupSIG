### 1.2 The PMMS→MBS→Treasury mapping: precise, with current numbers

This matters because it's your fair-value anchor if you decide to trade mortgage contracts.

**Freddie Mac PMMS methodology** [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, subagent read `freddiemac.com/pmms` directly]:
- Since Nov 17, 2022, PMMS is **not a lender survey** anymore — it's compiled from **administrative loan-application data** pulled from Freddie Mac's automated underwriting system (Loan Product Advisor), filtered to single-family, owner-occupied, conventional, 30/15-yr FRM, 75–80% LTV, FICO ≥740, conforming balance, lower-48 states.
- **Exact timing** (Freddie Mac's own words): *"The week of application activity starts at 12:00 a.m. ET the Thursday of last week and goes through 11:59 p.m. ET the following Wednesday... published weekly each Thursday at 12 p.m. ET."* This is exactly what Kalshi's contract rules cite, word for word, which I independently confirmed from Kalshi's own API in §1.1 above — strong cross-confirmation that the venues' contracts are faithfully tracking the primary source.
- **Quantified methodology noise**: Freddie Mac's own 2005–2021 backtest of legacy-survey-vs-new-method gives a mean difference of **6.6bp (std dev 7.4bp)** for the 30yr series. This is the *only* official noise figure available — Freddie Mac does not publish a week-to-week sampling error/confidence interval for the live print, which is a genuine, disclosed gap in the public methodology.

**The full chain, quantified with current data** [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, Dallas Fed/Fannie Mae/FRED]:
- Fannie Mae's own explainer: 30yr mortgage rate = 10yr Treasury + **secondary spread** (MBS yield minus Treasury, "reflects the additional risk present in an MBS") + **primary-secondary spread** (mortgage rate minus MBS yield, "reflects industry costs of originating a mortgage and lender margins").
- **Primary-secondary spread**: ~105bp average in 2022, ~120bp as a 20-year post-GFC average (Dallas Fed, `dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0507`, directly fetched by subagent), driven by servicing fees (25bp standard) + GSE g-fees + originator capacity/margin — widens specifically when refi demand spikes faster than origination capacity.
- **Secondary spread** (MBS-to-Treasury) is the bigger and more volatile piece — Dallas Fed attributes most of 2022's blowout to this leg, driven by the rising value of the borrower's **prepayment option** as rate volatility rose; a 2026 Dallas Fed regression finds **~70% of variance in mortgage-to-10yr spreads is explained by the level of 10yr rates, yield-curve slope, and implied rate volatility**.
- **Current snapshot I can give you concretely** [subagent, pulled from FRED's `fredgraph.csv` feed for `DGS10` and the OBMMI series, dated 2026-10-01/10-05]: 10yr UST 5.24%, OBMMI 30yr conforming 7.368–7.458% → **implied PMMS-to-10yr spread ≈ 204bp as of Oct 1, 2026** — well above the 60–190bp range documented even during 2022's volatility shock. If this holds up under your own verification, it's directly relevant to any CME OB30C spread-trade thesis, since CME explicitly markets that contract as a primary-secondary spread vehicle.
- **Lag, quantified concretely**: the real-time OBMMI index climbed from 7.014% (Sep 15) to 7.458% (Oct 5) — a 44bp move in three weeks — while the Thursday PMMS print published Oct 1 (covering the window ending Sep 30) still read 7.28%, already 9–18bp behind the real-time index by the time you'd see it. A trader relying only on the Thursday PMMS headline is structurally ~1 week behind a real-time rate-lock index. This is the central fact for anyone trying to build a fair-value nowcast for the PMMS-settled Kalshi/Polymarket contracts: **you need a same-day proxy (OBMMI, or the less-verified Mortgage News Daily index) to nowcast the number Freddie Mac hasn't published yet**, not the lagging PMMS print itself.

## 3. THE FAIR-VALUE ANCHOR

### 3.1 CME FedWatch — exact methodology [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, subagent read CME's own methodology article via Wayback Machine after live cmegroup.com access was blocked (Akamai bot protection, 403, confirmed across two independent attempts)]

CME's own worked formula, reproduced from "Understanding the CME Group FedWatch Tool Methodology" (CME Group, dated 2025-01-24):

1. **Base conversion**: `Implied Avg. EFFR = 100 − futures price`. (Example: ZQV2 price 96.9400 → implied rate 3.0600%.)
2. **Day-count apportionment** for a meeting month: `EFFR(Start)_T = {EFFR(Avg)_T − [(M/(M+N))×EFFR(End)_T]} / (N/(M+N))`, where N = days before the meeting, M = days after, in that calendar month.
3. **Probability extraction**: expected EFFR change over the meeting ÷ 25bp = a number whose integer part is the "floor" hike/cut size and whose decimal part ("mantissa") is the probability weight on the next-larger increment. CME's own worked example (Sept 21, 2022 meeting): this method gives P(50bp hike)=10%, P(75bp hike)=90% — I independently re-derived this arithmetic and it checks out exactly.
4. Successive meetings chain **multiplicatively** into a cumulative probability tree (the "conditional" view); a separate newer "aggregated" view instead compares future-month vs. current-month implied rates directly without compounding.

**CME's own stated limitations** (its exact words, from the "Assumptions"/footnotes sections): hikes/cuts are assumed uniformly 25bp and EFFR is assumed to react "proportionally"; FOMC meetings are assumed to occur on the published schedule; and critically, CME **admits** *"for every non-FOMC month there exists the possibility of a small discontinuity in the path of implied rates"* due to its own propagation convention. **CME's documentation does not itself discuss term premium, risk premium, or intermeeting moves** — those richer caveats come from independent Fed/academic research on the same instrument [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, Gürkaynak (2005) Fed Board working paper, read directly]:
- Term premium in fed funds futures estimated at **"about one to three basis points per month"** (Gürkaynak-Sack-Swanson 2002, cited within Gürkaynak 2005) — small but nonzero, uncorrected by CME.
- The model structurally treats intermeeting moves as **"zero probability events"** — it cannot price a surprise inter-meeting cut/hike by construction.
- **Liquidity has been shrinking and migrating to SOFR for two decades**, with three dated data points forming a clean trend: "liquid out to at least six months" (Gürkaynak 2005) → open interest "concentrated in the first nine months" (NY Fed, 2014) → "liquid enough to measure expectations roughly three months" (Fed Board FEDS WP 2024-034). CME's own ADV data for FY2025 confirms the magnitude of the shift: **SOFR futures+options ADV 5.4M contracts/day vs. Fed Funds futures 495K/day — an 11:1 ratio** [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, CME press release via Wayback].

**Is there a SOFR-equivalent FedWatch?** CME's own "SOFRWatch" is **not a probability tool** — it's a user-driven scenario calculator (you input a hypothesized rate path, it outputs implied futures prices) under a fixed EFFR–SOFR spread assumption. A genuine SOFR-**options**-based probability-distribution tool exists, but it's run by the **Atlanta Fed** ("Market Probability Tracker," using the Breeden-Litzenberger option-implied-density method), not CME.

### 3.2 Alternatives, ranked by what they actually measure

| Instrument | What it gives you | Main contamination | Liquidity (Sept 2026 CME ADV) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fed Funds futures | Discrete, meeting-indexed probability tree (FedWatch) | Small term premium (1–3bp/month); zero-probability intermeeting assumption; shrinking liquid horizon (~3mo) | not a headline CME product anymore |
| SOFR futures/options | Continuous-maturity point estimate (futures) or full probability distribution (options, via Atlanta Fed) | Similar term-premium issue, smaller in relative terms given deeper market | 4.6M contracts/day (+15% YoY) |

| Treasury futures/yields | Cruder, most liquid CME rates product by far | Term premium is large and currently *rising*: SF Fed's Christensen-Rudebusch model shows **10yr term premium at 1.49% of a 5.31% yield (28% of the total) as of 2026-10-05**, up from 1.16% a year earlier [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, directly-fetched live SF Fed data page, "Updated 10/6/2026"] | 9.5M contracts/day (+42% YoY) — the single most liquid CME rates product |

**Bottom line for your fair-value model**: Fed Funds futures/FedWatch are the cleanest *meeting-probability* signal but are now the thinnest of the three and only reliable to ~3 months out; SOFR futures+options are the liquidity leader and methodologically closest substitute; Treasury instruments are liquid but carry the most non-policy noise and that noise is **currently growing**, not shrinking — worth knowing if you're building a multi-year rate-path model today.

### 3.3 Is the anchor itself biased? Yes — and the bias has flipped sign since 2008

**Piazzesi & Swanson (2008)**, *Journal of Monetary Economics* 55(4) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, subagent read the published PDF directly, Table 1, sample Oct 1988–Dec 2005]: fed funds futures excess returns (futures rate minus realized rate) are **positive on average and rise with horizon** — 35.0bp/year at 1 month up to **61.4bp/year at 6 months** (t-stats 2.7–3.7) — meaning unadjusted futures **systematically overpredicted** the future funds rate, worst at longer horizons. The bias is strongly countercyclical: expected excess returns run **3–5x higher in recessions**, and a 100bp decline in payroll growth is associated with a 20bp (1-month)–73bp (6-month) rise in expected excess returns (R² up to 39% at 6 months). Their proposed correction (regress out own-rate + payroll growth) cuts mean forecast error roughly in half at long horizons (6-month RMSE: 66bp unadjusted → 50bp adjusted).

**Does it persist? The sign flipped.** Diercks & Carl (2019), Fed Board FEDS Notes [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, read directly]: the risk premium, positive pre-2008 per Piazzesi-Swanson, **"has been more negative since the financial crisis"** across multiple competing measures — their preferred estimate was ~−1bp/month (2019), implying a **12% (3-month) to 36% (9-month) upward adjustment** to market-implied hike probabilities once corrected. No peer-reviewed update extending this specific measure through the 2022-24 hiking cycle was found — this is a live gap in the literature, not just in our search. Hamilton (2009) partially complicates the picture: at very short (1-3 month), **daily**-frequency horizons, fed funds futures are "excellent predictors" and claims of a robust term premium are "sensitive to outliers" — reconcilable with Piazzesi-Swanson's result because Hamilton studies daily changes while Piazzesi-Swanson study multi-month holding-period excess returns.

**Practical implication for you**: if you build a model that treats CME FedWatch as ground truth, you are inheriting a documented, time-varying, business-cycle-correlated bias whose sign has flipped at least once in the last 20 years and whose current-regime magnitude nobody has rigorously re-estimated since 2019. That is itself a research gap you could fill, or at minimum a reason to never treat FedWatch as risk-neutral-equals-real-world without your own adjustment.

## 4. DOCUMENTED MISPRICINGS AND ARBITRAGE

### 4.1 Kalshi/Polymarket vs. futures-implied probability — no peer-reviewed study, but three credible, consistent sources

No academic journal article was found on this exact question. Three non-equivalent-tier sources agree on the qualitative pattern:

1. **Diercks, Katz & Wright, "Kalshi and the Rise of Macro Markets,"** Fed Board FEDS 2026-010 (Feb 2026, DOI 10.17016/FEDS.2026.010) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, subagent read the PDF directly] — official Fed staff working paper (not yet peer-reviewed), co-authored by Jonathan H. Wright (a leading macro-finance economist). Finds Kalshi's fed-funds-rate forecast has **"a perfect forecast record on the day before the FOMC meeting,"** a **"statistically significant improvement"** over the fed-funds-futures forecast; for CPI, Kalshi beats Bloomberg consensus on headline but not core; data covers 2022-2025.
2. **Dune Analytics blog, "Pricing the Fed: Kalshi, Polymarket, and CME FedWatch,"** Sept 10, 2025 [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] — **[non-peer-reviewed, methodologically transparent data blog]**. Across three 2026 "no change" FOMC decisions, Kalshi/Polymarket priced the hold **5-15¢ richer** than FedWatch at 1 meeting, up to **20¢+** at another, holding for up to 6 weeks, **converging to within 1-2¢ in the final 2-3 weeks**. The authors explicitly caution this is **not simple arbitrage** — Kalshi/Polymarket ask about a *specific meeting*, FedWatch reports probability of a *terminal rate marginalized across meetings*, different questions. Notional: ~$12M (Kalshi) and ~$232M (Polymarket) vs. "hundreds of billions" daily in fed funds futures.
3. **BMLL Technologies market-insight note**, Oct 1, 2026 [VERIFIED-PRIMARY] — **[institutional quant-data-vendor note, not academic, but order-book-data-driven]**. Using normalized order-book data across 12 FOMC meetings (May 2025–Sept 2026): median gap 2.9bp (July 2026) / 1.7bp (Sept 2026) between Kalshi and Fed Funds futures on a common DV01 scale, CME reacting **~0.6 seconds faster** than Kalshi to a CPI print, gap closing to 0.5-0.75bp within 20 minutes post-announcement. A toy cross-market hedge netted **$3,944 (July) / $791 (September)** — the authors themselves flag "two meetings are a small sample," but the existence of this note (from a firm that sells institutional order-book data) is itself evidence that **professional quant shops are already running this exact basis trade with better data than you currently have**.

Two SSRN papers (Krause, #7435839/#7442661) describing "arbitrage spreads up to ~12.25%" pre-decision were found only via search-engine synthesis — SSRN blocked direct access on every attempt — **flag as unverified, do not rely on this figure.**

**Read for your team**: the gap in the flagship Fed-decision contract is real but small (low single-digit bp in basis-point terms once properly scaled) and closes fast, and the entities already measuring it have institutional order-book data feeds you don't currently have. The gap is **structural (question-definition mismatch), not pure informational inefficiency** in at least one credible account — meaning a naive "buy the gap" strategy risks trading against a correct relative-value difference, not a mispricing.

### 4.2 Favourite-longshot bias in macro specifically — the critical finding

**This is the one result that should most directly shape your prioritization.** Le, "Decomposing Crowd Wisdom: Domain-Specific Calibration Dynamics in Prediction Markets," arXiv:2602.19520 (submitted Feb 2026, revised Aug 2026; replication code at `github.com/namanhzz/prediction-market-calibration`) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, subagent read the arXiv PDF directly] — 353 million trades across 429,000 Kalshi/Polymarket contracts, explicitly separating a "Finance" domain (nesting Fed/CPI/jobs/GDP contracts) from Politics/Sports/Weather/Entertainment/Crypto:

| Domain | ECE | MCE | Brier | Reliability (miscalibration) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Politics** | 0.117 | 0.315 | 0.119 | **0.024** |
| **Finance** | 0.016 | 0.037 | 0.156 | **0.000** |
| Sports | 0.008 | 0.025 | 0.185 | 0.000 |
| Entertainment / Weather / Crypto | 0.007–0.022 | 0.013–0.079 | 0.160–0.185 | 0.000–0.001 |

Direct quote: Politics' reliability component is **"five to fifteen times any other domain, all of which sit near zero."** This is a direct, quantified, out-of-sample (relative to your own election-market result) finding that **Kalshi/Polymarket's economic/finance contracts show essentially no measurable favourite-longshot bias**, sharply distinct from the severe, well-documented bias in their own election contracts — which is exactly consistent with your own sign-test results (p=3.6e-12, p=5.7e-19) being an **elections-specific**, not a general prediction-market, phenomenon.

One lower-confidence, SSRN-only, not-independently-readable counterpoint exists: Krause, "The Favorite-Longshot Bias in CPI Prediction Markets... Across Fed Rate Cycles" (SSRN #7093759) reportedly finds no significant bias pooled, but a **significant reversal by monetary regime** — longshot-overpricing during the 2022-23 hiking cycle (bias ≈ −0.049, p<0.001) flipping to longshot-*underpricing* in the subsequent pause/cutting cycle (bias ≈ +0.097, p=0.005) [**unverified from primary source** — present with appropriately lower confidence than the arXiv paper above]. If real, this says any bias in macro contracts is regime-dependent and sign-flipping, not a stable one-directional edge you could run unconditionally.

No dedicated study of Intrade's historical Fed-rate contracts or IEM's economic-indicator contracts (as distinct from their much-more-studied election contracts) was found — stated here plainly as a negative finding, not stretched from adjacent literature.

### 4.3 Structural frictions

- **Fees**: Kalshi's quadratic fee (`0.07×C×P×(1−P)`, peaking at the 50¢ strike) directly penalizes passive quoting near fair value — the point where your market-making edge is concentrated — more than quoting far out-of-the-money. The maker rate is exactly ¼ the taker rate, a real but partial offset.
- **Revisions**: Handled unusually cleanly and uniformly — every Kalshi macro contract I found explicitly resolves on the **first-published, non-preliminary** release, with revisions after expiration excluded by contract text. This removes one entire category of settlement-dispute risk you'd otherwise worry about with CPI/GDP/payrolls data.
- **Settlement disputes**: Kalshi reserves a discretionary "Market Outcome Review Process" (Rule 6.3/7.1) and, in the CORECPI rules specifically, the right to redesignate the Source Agency if its reliability is "impaired" — a real tail risk around a government-shutdown-driven data-release delay (there is a dedicated `KXNFPDELAY` contract for exactly this risk, which is itself informative about how often this has mattered recently).

## 5. WHAT IS DIFFERENT FROM ELECTIONS

**Resolution frequency**: FOMC 8x/year, CPI/jobs monthly, PMMS weekly, vs. one election date. This is a much faster repeated-game structure — more statistical power to calibrate a strategy quickly, but also recurring, correlated exposure to the *same* underlying factor every cycle rather than a fresh cross-section of independent races.

**Information arrival and passive market making through prints**: Kalshi's own contract rules make the danger explicit and quantified — CPI's last-trading time is **8:29am ET, expiring 8:31am ET, two minutes after the 8:30am BLS release**. That is not a window to react; it is a window in which your resting quotes get run over by the print if you haven't pulled them. Payrolls gives you a more generous 90-minute buffer (expiration 10:00am vs 8:30 release) — structurally different risk by contract family, worth encoding directly into your quoting logic rather than treating "macro contracts" as a single risk class. **Resting liquidity through a scheduled print is very likely a losing proposition by default** — exactly the options-market-maker playbook of widening or pulling quotes ahead of known-time news applies here, and the BMLL note's finding that CME reacts ~0.6 seconds faster than Kalshi to a CPI print tells you the professional participants are already doing this.

**Correlation structure**: your Poisson-Binomial/one-factor-copula infrastructure is directly reusable, and arguably *more* necessary here, not less — nearly the entire economics book (Fed decision, CPI, jobs, GDP, recession, mortgage rate) loads on a single latent "macro surprise" factor far more tightly than a basket of distinct congressional races ever would. The practical consequence: position-count-based risk limits are close to meaningless in this book; you need a single macro-factor exposure cap, sized against the plausible range of a surprise print (e.g., a hot CPI print moves Fed-decision, recession, *and* mortgage-rate contracts simultaneously in the same direction).

**Counterparties**: this is probably the most important structural difference and argues for caution. The Fed Board working paper (Diercks-Katz-Wright) finding Kalshi *beats* fed-funds-futures forecasts, and the BMLL note's description of a cross-market hedge converging within 20 minutes, both point to **informed, sophisticated flow** — academic researchers mining the data, and at least one data vendor already selling institutional clients tools to arb this exact basis. This is a different adverse-selection profile from retail political punters. Where your election edge (favourite-longshot bias) plausibly comes from behavioral biases in a retail-dominated flow, the macro book looks more like "professional rates traders using a cheaper venue to express a view they already have from their OTC book" — and §4.2's near-zero Finance-domain bias finding is consistent with that read.

## 6. HONEST ASSESSMENT

**Where you'd likely have an edge**: the newly-listed, thin, templated corners — mortgage-rate contracts specifically (weekly PMMS contract has real two-sided flow and a learnable OBMMI-based nowcast; the EOY range contract I observed firsthand had a 72-cent-wide, zero-volume market, i.e., either a gift or a market nobody wants, and you'd need to find out which), and plausibly the long-dated/recession corner of the book where institutional attention is thinnest. Your cross-venue fair-value skill transfers conceptually, but apply it to **venue pairs and contract families the professional shops haven't already wired up** (Kalshi vs. ForecastEx, Kalshi vs. the still-DCM-pending Limitless, or the mortgage contracts specifically, where no equivalent BMLL-style note exists yet) rather than Kalshi-vs-CME-FedWatch on the flagship Fed decision, where the gap is already small, fast-closing, and being harvested by people with better data than you.

**Where you'd be outgunned**: the Fed-decision and CPI flagship contracts. 1-cent-wide markets on millions of contracts of open interest, a Fed Board working paper already validating their forecast quality against the best professional benchmark, and a commercial order-book-data vendor already publishing basis-trade notes about them. Passive market making into a scheduled print here specifically is a bad idea by default — the structural 2-minute (CPI) or same-second (Fed decision) closing windows mean you are quoting into information events with no ability to react, against counterparties who already do this faster than you (CME beating Kalshi by 0.6 seconds, per BMLL).

**Is passive market making viable at all?** Plausibly, but narrowly: away from scheduled-print windows, in the wide/thin contracts (mortgage weekly and EOY, recession, long-dated GDP), where Kalshi's quadratic-fee-minus-maker-rebate structure (reaching a documented minimum net rate of ~1.75% of notional-at-risk at the 50¢ strike) is survivable against spreads you directly observed running into double-digit cents, not the 1-cent markets in the flagship contracts where professional-grade quoting has already compressed the edge below your proven ~+0.002/share benchmark.

**Ranking (edge × accessibility) / (effort + risk) — most to least attractive:**

1. **Kalshi mortgage-rate weekly contract (`KX30YMORTW`) market-making/nowcasting.** Accessibility: high (CFTC-regulated, public API, clean ToS fit for trading-on-Kalshi). Edge: plausible — new, thin, a learnable OBMMI/MND-based same-week nowcast against a lagging, administrative-data settlement source, real (if modest) two-sided volume already observed. Effort: low-moderate — reuses your existing market-making infrastructure; new work is building the PMMS nowcast model. Risk: contained (small single-name notional) but **does load on the same macro factor as everything else** — size it inside your existing factor cap, don't treat it as diversifying.
2. **Cross-venue basis in non-flagship contracts** (mortgage EOY range contracts across Kalshi/Polymarket; Fed/CPI across Kalshi vs. ForecastEx specifically, where no one appears to be publishing basis-trade research yet). Edge: unverified but plausible given thin coverage; accessibility: moderate (needs multi-venue connectivity and separate compliance review per venue — note the Kalshi ToS restriction does **not** bar you from building your own independent fair-value model and trading it on Kalshi *and separately* on another venue using that other venue's own data; it bars using *Kalshi's* API/data to inform trades elsewhere). Effort: moderate. Risk: moderate (venue/counterparty risk on less-established platforms, e.g., Limitless's DCM status is still pending).
3. **Recession and long-dated GDP contracts.** Similar thin/wide dynamic to mortgage EOY, less-studied than Fed/CPI, but also the hardest to build a genuinely differentiated fair-value model for (GDP nowcasting is a crowded, well-published field — Atlanta Fed's GDPNow is a public, sophisticated competitor you'd be benchmarked against for free).
4. **Flagship Fed-decision/CPI passive market making.** Low edge (already 1-cent markets, professionally arbed, Fed-paper-validated), high competition, explicit 2-minute pre-print closing windows that punish resting liquidity. **Not recommended as a core strategy**; at most, incidental flow.
5. **Directional longshot-bias bets on macro contracts** (the direct analogue of your best proven election edge). **Not recommended** — the best current evidence (Le 2026) says this specific bias is close to zero in the Finance domain, and the one source suggesting a macro-specific bias (Krause, unverified) describes it as regime-dependent and sign-flipping, not a stable exploitable edge.
