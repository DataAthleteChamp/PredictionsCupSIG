## 1. DOES THE FAVOURITE-LONGSHOT BIAS EXIST IN MACRO CONTRACTS?

### 1a. Is FLB domain-general, or sports/racing-specific? What does the literature say about financial/economic event contracts specifically?

**FLB is not a universal law — it is contested even within its home domain, and the handful of direct tests in financial markets point in different directions.**

- Ottaviani, M. & Sørensen, P.N., *"The Favorite-Longshot Bias: An Overview of the Main Explanations,"* working paper Nov. 2007 (published as a book chapter, *Handbook of Sports and Lottery Markets*, Elsevier 2008), https://web.econ.ku.dk/sorensen/papers/flbsurvey.pdf — **VERIFIED**. Footnote 1, in the authors' own words: *"A clear drawback of betting markets is that traders may be motivated by recreational objectives that we expect to play less of a role in regular financial markets."* This is the field's own canonical survey explicitly predicting the classic recreational/risk-love channel should be *weaker* in financial markets — but the survey is theory-only on betting markets and does not itself test financial data.
- Snowberg, E. & Wolfers, J., NBER WP 15923 (2010) / *Journal of Political Economy* 118(4):723-746 — **VERIFIED**. FLB is a strong regularity in racetrack betting worldwide but is **not universal even there**: Busche & Hall (1988) and Busche (1994) found no FLB in 2,653 Hong Kong races and 1,738 Japanese races. The paper never discusses financial markets at all — a real gap in the single most-cited modern FLB paper.
- Wolfers, J. & Zitzewitz, E., *"Prediction Markets,"* NBER WP 10504 (2004) / *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 18(2) — **VERIFIED, the most directly relevant primary source**. They explicitly bridge FLB to financial markets via the options volatility smile (citing Bates 1991; Rubinstein 1994) and ran a direct empirical test: Tradesports' binary-style S&P 500 year-end-range contract against the probability implied by actual CME S&P 500 options. Result: *"the extremely unlikely (high and low) outcomes for the S&P 500 are relatively over-priced on Tradesports... Similar patterns existed for Tradesports securities on other financial variables like crude oil, gold prices, and exchange rates. This finding is consistent with the longshot bias being more pronounced on smaller-scale exchanges."* **Important built-in caveat they flag themselves**: *"Aït-Sahalia, Wang, and Yared (2001) argue that the conclusion of miscalibration is less clear cut... because these prices may be driven by small likelihoods of extreme price changes"* — i.e., a standing academic objection that apparent longshot "mispricing" in financial tails may just be correctly-priced fat tails, not bias. (Aït-Sahalia et al. not independently fetched — second-hand citation.)
- Berg, J.E. & Rietz, T.A., *"Longshots, Overconfidence and Efficiency on the Iowa Electronic Market,"* draft 2018, forthcoming *International Journal of Forecasting* — **VERIFIED**: *"No longshot bias appears in IEM markets."* A real-money, long-running prediction market with no FLB at all.
- Cardozo, M. & Rivero-Wildemauwe, J.I., *"The Favorite-Longshot Bias in Prediction Markets: Evidence from Polymarket,"* arXiv:2609.12878 (Sept. 2026), https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12878 — **VERIFIED, full text read**. Using 588 million Polymarket trades (Nov 2022–Mar 2026): **Crypto and Politics show robust FLB under both weighting schemes. Sports is the clearest exception — longshots earn *positive* returns, the opposite of classic FLB.** Finance, Culture, Tech and Weather are aggregation-dependent: under equal-weighting, Finance shows *no significant* FLB (longshot return −2.6%, CI crossing zero); under dollar-weighting it shows large significant FLB (−72.4%), driven by concentration in a few large positions, not a broad pattern. **This is direct evidence that FLB in the "Finance" category of a major modern platform is not robust — it appears or disappears depending on how you count it.**

**Verdict on 1a: FLB is a real, well-replicated phenomenon in racetrack/sports betting but is explicitly flagged by its own theorists as likely weaker in financial contexts, is *not* universal even in sports, and the one large-sample direct test in a modern platform's "Finance" category found it only under one of two reasonable weighting conventions.** This alone should lower confidence that the team's politics-grade effect (p=3.6e-12/5.7e-19) simply carries over.

### 1b. Does FLB survive with financially sophisticated participants? Risk-love/misperception vs. structural explanations.

This is where the 2026 Kalshi-specific literature gets genuinely interesting and genuinely contradictory.

- **Formal theoretical result, peer-reviewed**: Whelan, K., *"On Prices and Returns in Commercial Prediction Markets,"* *Quantitative Finance* 23(11):1699-1712 (2023); working version https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/116926/1/MPRA_paper_116926.pdf — **VERIFIED**. Proves formally that trading **fees alone** mechanically generate FLB-shaped post-fee loss patterns **even with zero behavioral bias and symmetric, unbiased beliefs**. This matters enormously: it means an FLB-shaped *return* pattern on Kalshi does not by itself prove anyone is misperceiving probabilities — it could be a pure artifact of the fee formula (which, as shown in Section 2, is itself hump-shaped in price, i.e., structurally largest exactly where "probability" is most uncertain).
- **Direct empirical test against that theory, same lead author**: Bürgi, C., Deng, W. & Whelan, K., *"Makers and Takers: The Economics of the Kalshi Prediction Market,"* CESifo WP 12122 / SSRN 5502658 (Jan. 2026), https://www.karlwhelan.com/Papers/Kalshi.pdf — **VERIFIED, 300,000+ real Kalshi contracts**. Their own structural calibration finds the *opposite* of what pure-fee theory needs: **"a model with heterogeneous but unbiased beliefs cannot match the observed patterns... behavioral bias is necessary"** (modest calibrated bias, β=0.09). So Whelan's own 2023 theory paper and 2026 empirical paper are in direct tension — flagged explicitly, unresolved in the texts.
- **Does sophistication shrink it? Direct test, same paper.** Table 6 (by trading-volume quintile): *"there is no evidence of prices in higher-volume markets being more efficient predictors."* Table 7 (by average transaction size): **the quintile with the *largest* average trade size has the *largest* bias coefficient** — the opposite of the "big sophisticated money arbitrages it away" hypothesis. Table 9 (by calendar year 2021-2025): FLB is statistically significant in every single year, with only "some evidence of weakening" in 2025. **This is the single most direct test available of whether sophistication kills FLB on this exact platform, and it fails to find shrinkage.**
- **By category, same paper, Table 8 — directly on-point for macro**: *Financials* ψ=0.032 (p<0.01, n=27,123); *Economics* ψ=0.034 (p<0.01, n=24,405) — **both statistically significant and essentially identical in magnitude to the Kalshi-wide average (ψ=0.034), and larger than Politics (ψ=0.022, not significant at 5%)**. On its face this is the most encouraging finding in the whole report for the team's thesis: FLB-shaped losses exist in Kalshi's own macro categories at least as strongly as in politics.
- **Countervailing: calibration (not returns) methodology finds the opposite flavor of result.** Diercks, A.M., Katz, J.D. & Wright, J.H., *"Kalshi and the Rise of Macro Markets,"* Federal Reserve Board FEDS 2026-010 (Feb. 12, 2026), https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026010pap.pdf — **VERIFIED**. Using Probability Integral Transform uniformity tests on Kalshi-implied densities for CPI, unemployment, payrolls, GDP and fed-funds decisions: *"while there is some evidence of risk premia overstating the odds of higher inflation and higher unemployment, these probability density functions appear to be fairly well calibrated."* A materially milder conclusion than a strong FLB.
- **Unverified (SSRN-blocked) sub-studies disagree series-by-series**: Krause, D., multiple 2026 SSRN papers (7117919, 7087538, 7110758, 7442661) — **reported via secondary summary only, not independently confirmed; treat with real caution**. Per those summaries: CPI contracts specifically show **no** classic FLB ("efficient at the extremes"); unemployment contracts specifically show "clear evidence of the classic favorite-longshot bias" (longshots <$0.30 winning only 4.0% of the time vs. implied). Fed funds-rate markets reportedly "near-perfectly calibrated" (Brier 0.0001).

**Verdict on 1b: The sophistication-shrinks-it hypothesis is directly tested on this exact platform and fails — encouraging for persistence, discouraging for the "smart money will just arbitrage it instantly" fear. But three independent measurement approaches on overlapping 2026 Kalshi data (regression-based ψ-coefficients, PIT calibration, and series-specific Brier scores) reach three different-flavored conclusions, and none of this literature is peer-reviewed yet (all dated Jan–Sept 2026, all working papers or SSRN preprints).** This is not a settled question; it is a live, internally contradictory research frontier.

### 1d. Digital/binary option skew in FX and rates — the "digital risk reversal" analogy

This is the strongest, most rigorously quantified evidence in the whole report, and it cuts against easy optimism.

- Wystup, U. & Van Mulken, S., MathFinance AG, *"FX Column: OTC Currency Digital Contracts – Traded Price vs. Platform/Model Price,"* June 2023, https://www.mathfinance.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/2023-09_OTC-Currency-Digital-Contracts-Traded-Price-vs-Platform_Model-Price.pdf — **VERIFIED, named credentialed author** (Uwe Wystup, author of the standard Wiley FX options textbook). Mechanism: a digital is replicated as a tight vanilla call spread; because the smile is not flat, a single flat vol misprices it (the **"windmill effect"**). **Real case study**: a deep-OTM USD/JPY digital put (~−3% delta), identical contract, priced simultaneously at Bloomberg Black-Scholes-with-smile $168,300, MathFinance $152,000, Refinitiv Eikon $218,600 (flagged by the authors as wrong — missing the windmill adjustment), Bloomberg stochastic-local-vol $160,000, Bloomberg Vanna-Volga $188,000 — **a ~44% spread between the lowest and highest professional-platform prices for the same far-OTM digital.** They state directly: *"interbank bid-offer spreads for European digitals in USD-JPY are about 2-3% of the notional"* — for a digital whose own fair value is only ~3% of notional, **the bid-ask spread is roughly the same size as the contract's own mid-price.**
- Wystup, U., *"The market price of one-touch options in foreign exchange markets,"* Commerzbank Securities FX note (date approximate, early-to-mid 2000s), https://www.mathfinance.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/OT_derivativesweek.pdf — **VERIFIED**: *"low-TV [theoretical value] one-touch options can be twice as expensive, and sometimes even more expensive, than their TV."* The mechanism given is **"overhedge"** — the cost of vanna/volga hedging — explicitly **not** behavioral misperception.

**Verdict on 1d: among fully professional, competing, capitalized dealers, far-OTM digital/binary contracts still show large, persistent, "longshot-like" price discrepancies (up to 44% platform-to-platform) and wide spreads relative to fair value — for pure replication/hedging-cost reasons.** This is the single most important conceptual finding for the team: **if an FLB-shaped pattern survives in Kalshi macro contracts, the FX-digitals analogy says you should not assume it is free money sitting on the table for an outsider to pick up.** It may be the price market makers rationally charge for bearing a genuinely hard-to-hedge risk — the same economic logic Whelan's 2023 fee-mechanical paper derives for Kalshi specifically. A small team without inventory/hedging infrastructure is not obviously positioned to be the one who captures this, even if it is real.

## 2. IS PASSIVE MARKET MAKING VIABLE THERE?

### 2a. Kalshi's exact fee schedule and the arithmetic against a 0.4-tick edge

**VERIFIED from two independent primary sources four years apart** — Kalshi's live fee-schedule PDF (`kalshi.com/docs/kalshi-fee-schedule.pdf`, "Last updated and effective: July 7, 2026," accessed via proxy 2026-10-06) and Kalshi's CFTC self-certification filing (`cftc.gov/sites/default/files/filings/orgrules/22/09/rule091222kexdcm003.pdf`, filed Sept. 12, 2022) — the coefficients match across both:

> **Taker fee = round_up(M × 0.07 × C × P × (1−P))** dollars, M defaults to 1.
> **Maker fee** (introduced only after April 2025, confirmed independently by Bürgi/Deng/Whelan 2026, p.7: *"Kalshi began to charge fees on Makers after April 2025"*) **= round_up(M × 0.0175 × C × P × (1−P))** dollars, M defaults to **0** (i.e., free) *unless the series is listed in the "Non-Standard Fees" table*.

**The decisive, verified fact**: Kalshi's current Non-Standard Fees table lists **KXCPI, KXCPIYOY, KXFED, KXFEDDECISION, KXGDP, KXPAYROLLS, KXRATECUTCOUNT, and KXU3 — i.e., every macro series the team would trade — with Maker Multiplier = 1 (fee switched ON)**. I read the full alphabetical non-standard list and **found no presidential, Senate, House, or gubernatorial election ticker on it** — meaning flagship election markets get the plain default (**M=0, free** for resting/maker orders). Kalshi has, by its own published rulebook, made macro-contract liquidity provision strictly more expensive than election-contract liquidity provision.

**The arithmetic** (C=100 contracts, to avoid single-contract cent-rounding noise):

| P | Raw P(1−P) | Taker fee (0.07 coeff.) | Maker fee on macro (0.0175 coeff., M=1) | Maker fee on elections (M=0) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.50 | 0.2500 | **$1.75** → 1.75¢/contract | **$0.44** → 0.44¢/contract | $0.00 |
| 0.10 / 0.90 | 0.0900 | $0.63 → 0.63¢/contract | $0.16 → 0.16¢/contract | $0.00 |
| 0.01 / 0.99 | 0.0099 | $0.07 → 0.07¢/contract | $0.02 → 0.02¢/contract | $0.00 |

**Against the team's proven edge of $0.002/share (0.2¢/contract) at 5 minutes:**

- **At the money (P≈0.5, where market-making activity and uncertainty concentrate): the macro maker fee alone (0.44¢) is 2.2x the entire gross edge (0.2¢).** Net result: 0.2¢ − 0.44¢ = **−0.24¢/contract, net negative before the proven 30-minute decay even begins.** This is arithmetic, not inference: **the strategy is dead on arrival on Fed/CPI/GDP/jobs/unemployment contracts specifically**, exactly where Kalshi's own fee schedule turns the maker fee on.
- Away from the money (P=0.1/0.9): fee (0.16¢) consumes 80% of the gross edge, leaving a razor-thin 0.04¢ before spread/queue/adverse-selection costs — not obviously tradeable at any real size.
- Only deep in the tails (P=0.01/0.99) does the fee (0.02¢) fall meaningfully below the edge — but this is also where fill ratios collapse (per the team's own data) and where, as shown below, the FX-digitals analogy suggests spreads are structurally widest relative to fair value.
- **Separately and unconditionally**: the *taker* fee (0.07 coefficient, identical on both election and macro contracts — not differentiated) is 8.75x the edge at the money and 3.15x at P=0.1/0.9. This is consistent with — and probably explains — the team's own already-measured finding that at-the-touch markout is negative (−0.00559, P(>0)=0.000). That part of the economics hasn't changed between venues; what's new and adverse is specifically the maker-side fee on macro.

**Volume-tier/rebate programs**: **VERIFIED** — a tiered basis-point fee schedule (12.0bp down to 2.6bp taker) exists, but **only for crypto perpetual futures**, a separate product line (CFTC filing `rules0608265590.pdf`, filed June 8, 2026). **There is no equivalent volume discount for standard event contracts** — Fed/CPI/election contracts remain flat-rate regardless of trading volume. A formal "Market Maker Program" with fee rebates exists in the Kalshi Rulebook (Rules 4.1-4.5), but its numeric terms are filed confidentially, and a June 2025 CFTC filing states explicitly: *"The Program does not currently have any participants"* — suggesting Susquehanna's and Wintermute's liquidity provision (see 2e) sits outside this formal program as bilateral arrangements a small team could not access on the same terms.

### 2b. Spreads and depth on Fed/CPI contracts vs. the team's ~2.88-tick experience

**This is the weakest-evidenced section — no systematic study exists, only one anecdotal snapshot.** Bürgi, Deng & Whelan (2026), pp. 5-6, describe a real order-book screenshot of Kalshi's "April 2025 US CPI" market taken May 7, 2025 (six days pre-release): best Yes ask 32¢ with ~105 contracts available before stepping to 33¢ (~10,109 contracts); best Yes bid 28¢ — **a ~4¢ (4-tick) top-of-book spread with thin depth right at the touch (~100 contracts) but far deeper one tick away (~10,000 contracts).** This single data point suggests macro spreads may be at least as wide in raw tick terms as the team's 2.88-tick election experience, not tighter — and the thin top-of-book depth (~100 contracts) sits squarely in the team's own "fills well" bucket (under 80 fills 100%), meaning the team could get filled there, but would be competing for a very small, shallow slice of liquidity. **No source was found directly comparing Kalshi spread/depth statistics across macro vs. election categories** — flagged as a genuine evidence gap, not an oversight.

### 2c. Scheduled-release risk: does liquidity withdraw, do resting orders get picked off?

**No Kalshi-specific microstructure study of order-book behavior in the minutes around an FOMC/CPI release was found** — this is a real, load-bearing gap. Everything below is **extrapolated from adjacent, well-established markets**, not measured on Kalshi:

- Fleming, M.J. & Remolona, E.M., Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report 27, https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr27.html — **VERIFIED**, U.S. Treasury market: *"a notable lack of trading volume upon a major announcement when prices are most volatile. The bid-ask spread widens dramatically with price volatility and narrows just as dramatically with trading volume."*
- Ryu, Webb & Yu (SSRN 3963065) — **VERIFIED, full text read via proxy**, order-driven index-futures market: *"Fears of adverse selection significantly determine the bid-ask spread... pronounced around monetary policy announcements in particular."* This is squarely the "picking off" mechanism the question asks about — modeled and estimated directly — but in a Korean futures market, not Kalshi.
- Lucca, D. & Moench, E., *"The Pre-FOMC Announcement Drift,"* *Journal of Finance* 70(1):329-371 (2015) — **VERIFIED**: explicitly *"we find no such effect in U.S. Treasury securities and money market futures"* — a caution that pre-announcement behavior is asset-class-specific, not universal.
- One Kalshi-adjacent, Kalshi-rulebook fact **is** directly verified and relevant: Kalshi's own Exchange Notice (Appendix F, March 11, 2024, CFTC-filed) states that market makers' two-sided quoting obligations are time-bound, and *"pricing and liquidity outside of the required times may be worse than during the required times"* — i.e., **Kalshi itself acknowledges committed liquidity is not guaranteed at all times**, consistent with (though short of proof of) withdrawal around releases specifically.

**Verdict on 2c: the "picked off at the instant of release" risk is extremely well-documented in Treasury, FX and index-futures microstructure and is a standard, expected feature of scheduled-announcement markets generally — but it has not been directly measured on Kalshi's order book, and I flag that as an honest gap rather than asserting it as a Kalshi-proven fact.** Given how well-established this pattern is in every adjacent market studied, treating it as a real risk on Kalshi too is a reasonable precaution, not a stretch.

## 3. THE ANCHOR QUESTION

### 3a. Is fed funds futures / FedWatch an unbiased anchor? Piazzesi & Swanson (2008) and beyond.

**VERIFIED, full text read**: Piazzesi, M. & Swanson, E.T., *"Futures Prices as Risk-Adjusted Forecasts of Monetary Policy,"* *Journal of Monetary Economics* 55(4):677-691 (2008); NBER WP 10547; https://web.stanford.edu/~piazzesi/fff.pdf.

- **Magnitude**: fed funds futures **over-predict** the realized future funds rate on average by **35.0 to 61.4 basis points/year** across 1-6 month horizons (1988-2005 sample; post-1994 subsample slightly lower, 24.9-42.9bp/yr), rising monotonically with horizon.
- **Cyclicality — directly answers the question**: *"expected excess returns are about 3-5 times higher in recessions than they are on average during other periods"* (26.6-49.4bp non-recession vs. 77.8-177.0bp in recessions, by horizon). Employment growth is the strongest single predictor: *"a 1 percentage point drop in employment growth increases expected excess returns... by about 20-73 bp per year."* Mechanism, verbatim: *"right before recessions, when the Fed has already started easing, fed funds futures keep forecasting high funds rates"* — i.e., **the bias is largest and most dangerous exactly during the turning points a trader would most want to catch.**
- Cross-validated by Hamilton, J.D. & Okimoto, T., NBER WP 15736 (2010) / *Journal of Futures Markets* 31(3) (2011): independently estimates 32-73bp/year, "fairly consistent," concentrated in a high-volatility regime tied to weak growth/recession episodes.

**CRITICAL UPDATE the team needs and their prompt did not anticipate: the sign of this premium reverses after ~2000.**

- Diercks, A.M. & Carl, (2019), Federal Reserve Board FEDS Note, https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/simple-macro-finance-measure-of-risk-premia-in-fed-funds-futures-20190108.html — **VERIFIED**: *"starting in 2000, the average term premium flipped signs to negative one basis point per month"* (roughly −1bp/month, 3-9 month maturities), meaning **the unadjusted futures-implied path is now biased *low*, not high** — the opposite direction from Piazzesi-Swanson's headline full-sample number. A striking illustration they provide: as of April 2018, the market-implied probability of a 4th 2018 rate hike was **0% under a naive zero-premium read, 100% under one competing term-premium model (Priebsch 2017), and 35% under their own measure** — a 0-to-100 swing from methodology choice alone, on the same market data, same day.
- D'Amico, Kurakula & Sordo Palacios (2020), Chicago Fed Letter No. 432, https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/chicago-fed-letter/2020/432 — **VERIFIED**, independently corroborates: post-2004 premium "almost always negative, averaging about −1.5 and −1.6 basis points per month at the six- and 12-month horizons."

**What this means for "trade the gap": the anchor's bias is real, large, and well-documented — but it is not a stable, one-directional constant you can simply subtract off. It has flipped sign at least once in 35 years of data, among Fed-staff-grade researchers using different models on the same data.** A team porting over a "trade the gap vs. the external anchor" playbook that worked because election cross-venue biases are structurally stable (one venue is just persistently worse-calibrated) would be porting it into a setting where the comparable "bias" has already reversed direction once and could do so again without warning.

### 3b. CME FedWatch methodology: how does it handle multi-outcome meetings, and what breaks?

**VERIFIED directly from CME's own methodology page**, https://www.cmegroup.com/articles/2023/understanding-the-cme-group-fedwatch-tool-methodology.html:

> *"The probability of a rate hike (or conversely, a rate cut) is calculated by adding the probabilities of all target rate levels above (or below) the current target rate... assuming that rate hikes/cuts are uniformly sized in increments of 25bps."*

The mechanism: CME triangulates expected EFFR using **two adjacent contract months** (an "anchor month" with no FOMC meeting, propagated forward/backward), computes the implied number of 25bp moves for a meeting month (e.g., 2.9103), then **splits this into integer + fractional ("mantissa") parts**: P(smaller move) = 1 − mantissa, P(next-larger move) = mantissa. **This confirms exactly what the question hypothesized: the tool can only ever produce exactly TWO adjacent outcomes per meeting from a single pair of futures prices — never three or more.** CME's own disclosed limitation: *"Calculated probabilities are estimates based on these assumptions and may be subject to variation if any tenet is compromised."*

**Concrete, recent, documented distortion** — Diercks, Katz & Wright (Fed, Feb. 2026), same paper as above, Section 5, "Kalshi vs Fed Funds Futures": *"In contrast, fed funds futures require binomial tree assumptions which enforce just two possible outcomes."* Their dated examples: **September 2025 FOMC** — futures implied a binary 75%-on-a-cut vs. no-change, but Kalshi's richer distribution showed this 75% was "likely lumping in weight on lower rate outcomes" (hiding bigger-cut probability mass inside the "cut" bucket). **October 2025 FOMC** — *"Kalshi market implies there is nonzero weight on seven different outcomes. When we instead use Fed Funds futures, we have to make an assumption of just two possible outcomes, which clearly implies too little uncertainty."* This is not noise — it is a structural misspecification that is worst exactly when the Fed's reaction function is most genuinely uncertain, which is also when a "trade the gap" strategy would be most tempted to act.

### 3c. Measured Kalshi-vs-FedWatch gaps: risk premium, or just a different question?

- Dune (data analytics), https://dune.com/blog/pricing-the-fed-kalshi-polymarket-cme-fedwatch (accessed Oct 6, 2026) — **VERIFIED**: peak gaps of 5-20+ percentage points months ahead of a meeting, converging to 1-2pp in the final weeks. Their own causal read: *"That gap isn't the crowd beating the bond market. It's the two markets answering different questions"* — Kalshi prices the single meeting conditional on the path so far; FedWatch's "aggregated" view marginalizes over all intermediate meetings. **A meaningful share of "the gap" is a definitional artifact, not a mispricing at all.**
- BMLL (named analysts: Etienne Mercuriali, Ben Bingham), https://www.bmlltech.com/news/market-insight/when-kalshi-and-fed-funds-futures-disagree (Oct. 1, 2026) — **VERIFIED**: across 12 FOMC meetings (May 2025-Sept 2026), gaps of up to 2.8bp in rate terms. July 2026: median 2.88bp, Kalshi closer to the actual outcome (hold). September 2026: median 1.68bp, futures closer (actual 25bp hike). **"Earlier meetings show no consistent sign."** Their own toy hedge strategy found modest profit ($3,944 / $791) but explicitly caveat: *"Two meetings are a small sample."*

**Verdict on 3: the anchor is demonstrably not a clean, stable reference. Part of the "gap" is structural/definitional (not arbitrageable), part is a risk premium whose sign has flipped historically and whose current sign nobody has freshly re-estimated for 2026, and the one rigorous recent measurement found no consistent directional sign meeting-to-meeting.** This is a materially weaker, noisier basis for a systematic strategy than the team's longshot-fade edge, which is strongly one-directional (p=3.6e-12/5.7e-19). Trading "the gap" on rates risks trading noise dressed up as a risk-premium story.

## 4. CORRELATION AND PORTFOLIO RISK

**The team's own framing (rho≈0.25 for a national election swing factor) versus rates is not a close comparison — it understates how much worse the rates case is.**

**Direct, verified structural evidence that meetings are not independent draws**: CME's own FedWatch methodology, confirmed above, builds its multi-meeting **"Conditional"** view by **compounding sequential binary meeting-probabilities** — explicitly a path/Markov-chain structure where this meeting's outcome mechanically re-sets the starting point and conditional distribution for the next (CME introduced a second, "Aggregated," view in 2024 specifically because the Conditional view's path-dependence was itself a source of confusion — https://www.cmegroup.com/articles/2024/the-cme-fedwatch-tool-aggregated-enhancement-explained.html, June 2024, **VERIFIED**). Kalshi's own product design encodes the same logic: a "KXRATECUTCOUNT" contract (number of rate cuts across a full year) packages the whole meeting sequence into one instrument rather than pretending each meeting is separable.

**Quantitative proxy for just how dominant the common factor is**: Litterman, R. & Scheinkman, J., *"Common Factors Affecting Bond Returns,"* *Journal of Fixed Income* 1(1):54-61 (1991) — a canonical, extremely widely-cited finding (cross-verified across multiple independent citing sources; I was not able to extract clean full-text from the primary PDF due to a tool/parsing failure, flagged as lower-confidence sourcing than my other citations, though the figure is about as uncontested as any number in fixed-income finance): **the first principal component ("level") of Treasury returns across the maturity spectrum explains ~89.5% of total variance**, with slope (~8-9%) and curvature (~2-3%) a distant second and third.

**My own derivation from this** (standard portfolio algebra, not itself a cited finding — shown explicitly so it can be checked): for N equally-weighted, equally-correlated unit-variance bets with pairwise correlation ρ, the variance of their average is Var = ρ + (1−ρ)/N, and the "effective number of independent bets" N_eff → 1/ρ as N grows. For elections, ρ≈0.25 → **N_eff → 4** (a real, if limited, diversification benefit across a 50-state book). For an equicorrelated system where one factor explains a fraction R² of total variance, the large-N relationship is R² → ρ — so an ~89.5% level factor implies an **effective ρ_rates on the order of 0.85-0.90**, giving **N_eff → ~1.1-1.2** — i.e., **a book of many FOMC-meeting or maturity-point contracts carries risk equivalent to roughly one single position, not many.** (Caveat, stated plainly: this reuses a *cross-sectional*, same-day, across-maturity correlation structure as a proxy for *across-time*, across-meeting dependence — a reasonable analogy, not an identical measurement, because the same underlying force — persistent shifts in the expected policy path — drives both. It is supplemented, not replaced, by the directly-on-point CME path-dependence evidence above.)

I also attempted to independently verify Rudebusch, G.D., *"Term Structure Evidence on Interest Rate Smoothing and Monetary Policy Inertia,"* *Journal of Monetary Economics* (2002), as further support, but my fetch of the primary PDF returned unparseable binary/font data rather than clean text — **I could not independently confirm this one**, and note that secondary summaries describe a more nuanced finding than "meetings are mechanically linked": Rudebusch argues that *apparent* policy inertia is partly an illusion driven by persistent underlying economic shocks rather than deliberate Fed smoothing, and that term-structure evidence shows *less* predictability than naive high-inertia models would imply. I'm flagging this honestly rather than citing it as unambiguous support — **whatever the mechanism (deliberate smoothing vs. serially correlated shocks), the empirically observed historical pattern of multi-meeting hiking cycles (2015-2018, 2022-2023) and cutting cycles (2024-2025, per the Fed's own September 16, 2026 statement raising the target range to 3.75%-4.00% after an easing phase — direct primary-source read, https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm, accessed 2026-10-06) is well known and not in dispute; only the deeper causal interpretation is contested.**

**Verdict on 4: there is essentially no meaningful diversification available within a rates book. A portfolio of Fed-meeting or maturity-point contracts is much closer to one concentrated bet on the path of policy than to a basket of many quasi-independent bets** — a qualitatively different risk profile than the 50-state election book (N_eff≈4) the team's current risk limits and position-sizing were presumably calibrated against. Reusing election-book position sizing on a rates book would understate true risk by something like a factor of 3-4x on this analysis.

### Single biggest reason this would fail
**Arithmetic, not inference.** Kalshi's own published, CFTC-filed fee formula makes macro contracts strictly more expensive to quote passively than the election contracts the team's edge was proven on, by an amount (0.44¢ vs. a 0.2¢ edge) that flips the trade negative before any other consideration. Layer the documented, named, institutional market-maker presence (Susquehanna, Wintermute, Jump Trading) concentrating in exactly this category on top of that, and the case against the team's flagship technique is about as close to dispositive as due diligence gets without a live account.

### Single most promising niche if anything would work
Not market-making. If anything survives, it is a **slow, hold-to-settlement fade of mispriced deep-tail macro contracts** — sized into the part of Kalshi's fee curve where the formula charges <0.1¢/contract (deep out-of-the-money strikes, away from the at-the-money zone where both fees and institutional competition are worst) — because the one Kalshi-specific finding that actually supports transfer (Bürgi/Deng/Whelan 2026) is a *returns*-based fade finding that explicitly does not shrink with trade size, not a market-making finding. This is a hypothesis to test cheaply, not a validated edge: the contradictory, unverified Krause sub-studies (no bias in CPI specifically, clear bias in unemployment specifically) mean it must be re-established series-by-series before any capital is committed.

### Is the honest answer "do not bother"?

**For passive market making and for the naive cross-venue/FedWatch trade: yes, plainly, do not bother.** The fee schedule and the risk-premium/methodology literature are solid enough, from primary sources, that further live testing would mostly spend capital to re-confirm what Kalshi's own rulebook and the Federal Reserve's own economists have already shown in writing.

**For the favourite-longshot fade: not quite "don't bother," but close to it, and conditional.** It is worth a **small, bounded, capital-free research sprint** — days, not months — to directly replicate the team's own politics-grade sign-test methodology on fresh Kalshi macro trade/order data, *net of the verified maker/taker fees*, series by series (CPI vs. unemployment vs. Fed-funds vs. GDP separately, since the contested literature disagrees precisely at that level of granularity). What would have to be true to turn this into a real "go":

1. The replicated sign-test, run independently by the team (not inherited from the 2026 working papers), produces p-values in the team's own politics-grade range (1e-12 to 1e-19), not the more marginal coefficients (ψ≈0.03) found in the one rigorous study available.
2. The effect survives **after** fully netting Kalshi's verified fee schedule — a concrete, bright-line, already-computable hurdle.
3. The effect is concentrated in specific contracts/tenors where direct order-book inspection shows institutional two-sided quoting is thin — not simply inherited at the category level, where Susquehanna/Wintermute are known to be active.
4. Any cross-venue/anchor component is rebuilt from a continuously re-estimated, regime-aware premium (not a static historical number), explicitly because the historical premium has already changed sign once.
5. Portfolio/position sizing is rebuilt from the near-single-factor reality of a rates book (effective N≈1-1.2), not carried over from the election book's N≈4 diversification assumption.

If the team's actual constraint is calendar time rather than a few analyst-days, the pragmatic shortcut is simply: **don't bother at all** — three of the four techniques transfer clearly negatively, the fourth is contested enough that it should not receive meaningful resources without a cheap first-party check, and nothing in this research surfaced a reason to expect the kind of overwhelming, p=1e-12-grade edge in macro contracts that would justify months of the team's time on priors alone.
